# The Lean Startup, Chapter 12: Innovate

> Part Three: ACCELERATE ｜ PDF p.244-261

## 中文版

### 一句话总结

公司变大以后照样可以持续创新，前提是给内部创业团队搭好正确的结构，用 innovation sandbox 让实验公开进行，同时保护母公司，并把 entrepreneur 当成一个正式的职业角色来管理。

### 核心观点

1. **公司变大不等于失去创新能力。** 常见看法是公司越大越不会创新，作者不同意。他认为成长中的 startup 和成熟的大公司都可以学会同时照顾现有客户、寻找新客户、管理现有业务、探索新的 business model。他把这种思路叫做 *portfolio thinking*。(p.244)

2. **内部创业团队需要三种结构条件。** 作者认为不管是独立 startup 还是公司内部团队，都需要：资源少但稳定、独立的开发权限、对结果有个人利害关系。结构对了不保证成功，但结构错了几乎一定失败。(p.244)
   - **Scarce but secure resources**：大公司部门习惯多要预算，被砍 10% 也能扛过去。Startup 不一样，钱太多和太少一样有害，而且中途被砍一部分可能就是致命打击。所以 startup 需要的钱少，但这笔钱必须不能被随意动。(p.245)
   - **Independent development authority**：团队要能自己设计和执行实验，不用层层审批。作者建议团队完全 cross-functional，每个相关部门都有全职成员，能做出真正可以交付给客户的产品。交接和审批越多，Build-Measure-Learn 循环越慢。(p.245-246)
   - **A personal stake in the outcome**：独立创业靠股权，内部团队如果用奖金，最好和创新产品的长期表现挂钩。利害关系也可以是非金钱的，比如明确这个产品是谁做出来的，让功劳归到创新者名下。Toyota 的 *shusa*（chief engineer）对一款新车从头到尾拥有最终决定权，员工甚至把车称作 shusa 的车。(p.246-247)

3. **反面例子：奖励规则说不清。** 作者提到一家以创新文化出名的科技公司，新产品成绩却很差。它会给做出非凡成绩的团队发大额奖励，但由高层决定，标准没人知道。团队不相信自己能长期拥有创新成果，于是不愿冒真正的风险，只做讨高层喜欢的项目。(p.247)

4. **一场失败的定价实验会议。** 作者的一个大公司客户做了定价实验，开会时没人看得懂数据是怎么来的，也不确定哪些客户参与了实验，实验做了好几个月，设计和执行的人还分到了不同部门。最后各部门各自挑对自己有利的解读，决策还是靠谁的论点听起来更有道理。作者指出问题包括 vanity metrics、周期太长、batch 太大、假设不清、没有团队 ownership。这种会议让公司里的人都开始反感"数据驱动"和实验。(p.247-249)

5. **阻挠来自合理的恐惧。** 这家公司主要收入来自 B2B 企业客户，增长在放缓；消费者市场潜力大，但公开标价对个人用户太高。负责消费者市场的团队想试更低的价格，负责企业客户的团队担心老客户发现个人买得更便宜，影响现有关系。于是每个实验都被拖延、破坏或搅浑。作者强调这种反应是合理的，成熟公司确实有很多东西可以失去。(p.249-250)

6. **把创新藏进 black box 很危险。** 不创新，公司迟早会走向 *The Innovator's Dilemma* 描述的结局。作者把常见问题反过来问：怎样保护母公司不受 startup 伤害？秘密的 skunkworks 团队偶尔会成功，比如 IBM 在 Boca Raton 单独开发最初的 PC，但这类做法很少带来可持续的创新。被蒙在鼓里的经理会觉得被背叛，之后只会更热衷于政治斗争。作者认为问题出在高层没有设计好支持创新的系统。(p.250-251)

7. **Innovation sandbox 的规则。** 作者的方案是建一个 sandbox，限制实验的影响范围，但不限制团队的方法。规则包括：任何团队都可以做只影响 sandbox 部分或特定客户群的 split test；一个团队负责实验全程；实验有时间上限和客户数量上限；每个实验用一份 5 到 10 个 actionable metrics 的标准报告评估；所有团队用同样的指标；团队要实时监控指标和客户反应，出现严重问题就中止。(p.252)

8. **Sandbox 从小开始，客户是真实的。** 上面那家公司最初的 sandbox 只包括定价页面。线上服务可以限定某些页面或用户流程，零售可以限定某些门店或地区，新产品可以限定某些客户群。和概念测试不同，sandbox 里的客户是真客户，团队可以和他们建立长期关系。(p.252-253)

9. **Sandbox 带来的好处。** 真实验的结果很清楚，指标要么动了要么没动。因为大家反复用同一套指标汇报，整个公司对 actionable metrics 的理解会提高，就算有人想阻挠创新团队，也得先学会这些指标。小 batch 和全程负责让团队能快速得到反馈，即使一开始想法很差，也能较快找到好的方案。(p.253-254)

10. **内部团队的问责方式。** 和第 7 章讲的 learning milestones 一样：先根据 customer archetypes 建立理想模型，推出 MVP 建立 baseline，再 tune the engine 向理想靠近。团队证明成功后，要整合进公司整体的产品组合。(p.254)

11. **四类工作与 Entrepreneur 这个头衔。** 公司要同时管理四类工作：新产品的研发、扩张和规模化、市场成熟后的优化与降本、以及以外包和自动化为主的旧产品维护。每个成功的产品都会经历这些阶段。问题是发明者常常跟着产品走，结果有创造力的经理被困在增长和优化工作里。作者建议每个阶段有自己的 cross-functional 团队，产品在阶段之间交接，员工可以选择跟着走或留下来做新东西。在大公司里，创业应该是一条正式的职业路径，名片上可以直接写 "Entrepreneur"，用 innovation accounting 来考核和晋升。(p.254-256)

12. **Sandbox 会成长，最终也会被自己的成功困住。** 产品孵化成功后要回到母公司，由更大的团队扩张，一开始仍需原创新者带领，他们正好可以把新的工作方式教给新成员。Sandbox 也可以逐步扩大，比如从定价页扩到首页、搜索功能。但当产品占满整个 sandbox，它就会背上关键业务需要的各种规则，创新者变成现状的守护者，新团队又需要一个新的 sandbox。(p.256-257)

13. **创新者变成 status quo 之后。** 作者早年在公司里总是推动快速迭代和数据驱动，由于 anchoring 效应，他的提议越激进，最后的折中就越接近他真正想要的。后来他自己负责产品开发，员工也用同样的方式向他提各种相反的建议。他的做法是既不教条地拒绝，也不机械地取中间值，而是用科学方法检验：能否用理论预测结果？能否在小团队里试？能否衡量影响？他也提到 IMVU 的很多 Lean Startup 做法其实来自员工。(p.258-259)

14. **需要理论，而不是照搬步骤。** 把 Lean Startup 当成固定步骤来执行的人不会成功。出了问题时，要像 Deming 说的那样区分 special cause 和 systemic cause，这需要理论。比如把团队的生产力标准从职能表现换成 validated learning，一开始感觉一定会变差，因为旧系统的问题看不见，新系统的问题很明显。重要的是整个循环有多快，不是某一个环节多快。提前设定预期，用 Five Whys 等方法调整。Lean Startup 是一个框架，不是蓝图。作者建议加入实践社群，下一章列出了资源。(p.259-261)

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Companies can keep innovating as they grow if they give internal startup teams the right structure, run experiments in the open through an innovation sandbox that also protects the parent company, and treat entrepreneur as a real job with its own career path.

### Key Points

1. **Getting bigger does not have to kill innovation.** The common view is that large companies lose the ability to innovate. Ries disagrees. He argues that growing startups and established firms can learn to serve existing customers, find new ones, run current lines of business, and explore new business models at the same time. He calls this *portfolio thinking*. (p.244)

2. **Internal startup teams need three structural attributes.** Whether independent or internal, startup teams need scarce but secure resources, independent authority to develop their business, and a personal stake in the outcome. The right structure does not guarantee success, but the wrong one almost guarantees failure. (p.244)
   - **Scarce but secure resources**: Division leaders in big companies grab as much budget as they can and can survive a sudden 10 percent cut. Startups are different. Too much money hurts as much as too little, and a midcourse cut can be fatal. They need less capital, but it has to be safe from tampering. (p.245)
   - **Independent development authority**: Teams must be able to design and run experiments without piling up approvals. Ries recommends fully cross-functional teams with full-time members from every relevant department, able to ship real, working products to customers. Handoffs and approvals slow the Build-Measure-Learn loop. (p.245-246)
   - **A personal stake in the outcome**: Independent startups use equity. Internal teams that rely on bonuses should tie them to the innovation's long-term performance. The stake can also be nonfinancial, such as making clear who the innovator is and giving that person credit. At Toyota, the *shusa* (chief engineer) has final authority over a new vehicle from start to finish, and employees call the car the shusa's car. (p.246-247)

3. **A counterexample: rewards with no clear rules.** Ries describes a well-known tech company with an innovative reputation and a weak record of new products. It gives large awards to teams that do something extraordinary, but senior management decides on unknown criteria. Teams do not expect to own their innovations, so they avoid real risk and pursue projects that will please executives. (p.247)

4. **A pricing experiment meeting gone wrong.** At one large client, senior managers met to review a pricing experiment. Nobody understood how the numbers were derived or which customers had been exposed. The experiment took months, and the people who designed it had been moved away from those who ran it. Each department picked the reading that suited it, and the executive ended up deciding on the most plausible argument. Ries lists the problems: vanity metrics, long cycle time, large batches, an unclear hypothesis, weak design, and no team ownership. Meetings like this gave data-driven decisions a bad name inside the company. (p.247-249)

5. **The sabotage came from a rational fear.** Most of the company's revenue came from B2B enterprise sales, where growth was slowing. The consumer segment had potential, but the published list price was too high for individuals. The consumer team wanted to test lower prices. The enterprise team worried that corporate clients would find out individuals paid less. So every experiment was delayed, sabotaged, or obscured. Ries stresses that this fear is reasonable, since an established company has a lot to lose. (p.249-250)

6. **Hiding innovation in a black box is risky.** Without agile experimentation, the company would face the fate described in *The Innovator's Dilemma*. Ries flips the usual question and asks how to protect the parent organization from the startup. Secret skunkworks teams sometimes succeed once, like IBM building the original PC in Boca Raton, but they rarely lead to sustainable innovation. Managers who get surprised feel betrayed and become more political. Ries puts the blame on senior executives who did not design a supportive system. (p.250-251)

7. **The rules of the innovation sandbox.** Ries proposes a sandbox that limits the impact of an innovation without limiting the team's methods. Any team can run a true split test that affects only the sandboxed part of the product or certain customer segments. One team sees each experiment through end to end. Experiments have a time limit and a cap on how many customers they touch. Each is judged by one standard report of five to ten actionable metrics, and every team uses the same metrics. Teams must watch metrics and customer reactions during the test and stop it if something goes badly wrong. (p.252)

8. **Start small, with real customers.** In the company above, the first sandbox covered only the pricing page. An online service might limit it to certain pages or user flows, a retailer to certain stores or regions, and a new product to certain customer segments. Unlike a concept test, customers in the sandbox are real, and the team may build long-term relationships with them. (p.252-253)

9. **What the sandbox gives you.** True experiments are easy to judge because top-level metrics either move or they don't. Reporting with the same metrics every time builds metrics literacy across the company, so even someone who wants to sabotage the team has to learn actionable metrics first. Small batches and end-to-end ownership give fast feedback, so teams converge on good solutions quickly even when they start with bad ideas. (p.253-254)

10. **Holding internal teams accountable.** The sequence matches the learning milestones from Chapter 7: build an ideal model based on customer archetypes, launch an MVP to set a baseline, then tune the engine toward the ideal. Teams that show success must then be folded into the company's product portfolio. (p.254)

11. **Four kinds of work, and Entrepreneur as a job title.** Companies manage four kinds of work: creating new products, scaling them, optimizing and cutting costs once the market matures, and running legacy products through outsourcing and automation. Every successful product passes through these phases. The trouble is that inventors often follow their products, so creative managers get stuck in growth and optimization work. Ries suggests a cross-functional team for each phase, with products handed off between them and people free to move with the product or start something new. Entrepreneurship should be a real career path inside large companies, with "Entrepreneur" on the business card and promotion based on innovation accounting. (p.254-256)

12. **The sandbox grows, and eventually success catches up with it.** After incubation, a product goes back to the parent company to be scaled by a larger team, led at first by the original innovators, who can teach new members the new way of working. The sandbox can also expand, for example from the pricing page to the home page and then search. Once the product fills the whole sandbox, it picks up the rules of a mission-critical operation. The innovators become guardians of the status quo, and new teams need a new sandbox. (p.256-257)

13. **When the innovator becomes the status quo.** Early in his career, Ries pushed hard for rapid iteration and data-driven decisions. Because of anchoring, the more radical his proposal, the closer the compromise landed to what he wanted. Later, when he ran product development, his own employees used the same tactic with opposite suggestions. His answer was neither to reject them dogmatically nor to split the difference, but to test them: can theory predict the result, can a small team try it, can we measure the impact? He notes that many Lean Startup practices at IMVU came from employees. (p.258-259)

14. **You need theory, not a checklist.** People who treat the Lean Startup as a fixed set of steps will fail. When things go wrong, you have to tell a special cause from a systemic one, as Deming put it, and that takes theory. Switching a team's measure of productivity from functional excellence to validated learning will feel worse before it feels better, because the old system's problems are hard to see and the new system's problems are obvious. What counts is how fast you get through the whole loop. Set expectations early and adapt with tools like the Five Whys. The Lean Startup is a framework, not a blueprint. Ries recommends joining a community of practice, covered in the last chapter. (p.259-261)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Portfolio thinking | Managing existing business, new customers, and new business models at the same time |
| Scarce but secure resources | A small budget that is protected from midcourse cuts |
| Shusa | Toyota's chief engineer, with final authority over a new vehicle's development |
| Innovation sandbox | A bounded area where experiments have limited impact but teams keep full freedom of method |
| Skunkworks | A secret innovation team kept separate from the parent company |
| Actionable metrics | Metrics that show cause and effect and guide decisions, as opposed to vanity metrics |
| Four kinds of work | New product creation, scaling, optimization and cost cutting, and legacy maintenance |
| Special cause vs. systemic cause | Deming's distinction between problems from one-off factors and problems built into the system |

### Discussion Questions

- In an organization you know, how are the innovation team's budget, authority, and incentives set up? Which of the three attributes is missing?
- If you were introducing a sandbox at a large company, what is the smallest scope you would start with, and why?
- Ries says switching to validated learning feels worse before it feels better. How would you set that expectation with your team and your boss so the project is not cancelled halfway?
