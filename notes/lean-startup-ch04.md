# The Lean Startup, Chapter 4: Experiment (Startup Experiments)

> Part One: VISION ｜ PDF p.63-77

## 中文版

### 一句话总结

把 startup 的每一步都当成科学实验来做：先写出清楚的假设，用小规模、低成本的产品去测试真实客户行为，这样既能尽早发现错误，又不会牺牲长期愿景。

### 核心观点

1. **"Just do it" 式创业学不到东西。** 很多团队的计划是"先把产品发出去，看看会怎样"。作者说这样的团队一定能"看到会发生什么"，但不一定得到 validated learning。科学方法的一条重要原则是："if you cannot fail, you cannot learn"。没有一个可能被证伪的预测，就谈不上学习。(p.63)

2. **从炼金术到科学。** Lean Startup 把 startup 的工作看成一系列实验，用来检验战略里哪些部分是对的、哪些是错的。真正的实验要先有明确的 hypothesis，并对结果做出预测，再用实际数据检验。科学实验由理论指导，startup 实验由愿景指导，目标是找出如何围绕这个愿景建立可持续的业务。(p.63-64)

3. **Zappos：想得大，从小处做。** 创始人 Nick Swinmurn 没有先建仓库、找分销伙伴，而是去本地鞋店给库存拍照放到网上，有人下单就回店里按原价买下再寄出。这个实验要回答的核心问题是：人们是否已经愿意在网上买鞋。同时，因为要收款、处理退货、做客服，它顺带检验了很多其他假设。和问卷调查相比，它的好处有三点：观察的是真实购买行为而非假设性回答；能和真实客户互动，了解他们的需求；会被客户意料之外的行为"惊到"，比如退货。后来 Zappos 据报道以 12 亿美元被 Amazon 收购。(p.64-65)

4. **HP 的志愿者项目也是一个 startup。** HP 允许员工每月用最多四小时工作时间做志愿服务，但大多数员工不知道这项政策，参与的人也多是做体力活，没有发挥专业技能。负责人 Caroline Barlerin 按传统方式做了 18 个月的路线图和未来四年的考核指标，却只取得了一些零散的成功，仍然不知道愿景能否规模化。作者认为，这个项目面临极高的不确定性，计划里充满未经检验的假设，所以适合用 Lean Startup 的方法。(p.65-67)

5. **拆解愿景：value hypothesis 和 growth hypothesis。** 创业者最重要的两个假设是：
   - **Value hypothesis**：产品在客户使用后是否真的带来价值。与其问员工感受如何，不如看参加过志愿活动的员工有多少会再报名，用 retention 作为价值的代理指标。
   - **Growth hypothesis**：新客户如何发现这个产品。如果项目靠口碑传播，就要衡量早期参与者是否真的主动拉同事加入。(p.67-68)

6. **从 early adopters 开始，测行为而不是问意见。** 实验可以只选十几位老员工，专门找那些最强烈感到日常工作与公司价值观脱节的人，因为 early adopters 最需要这个产品，也更能容忍瑕疵、更愿意给反馈。再用 concierge MVP（第 6 章详细讲）给他们提供尽可能好的体验，然后看有多少人完成任务、再次参加、愿意拉同事。如果十个人都不愿再来，这就是非常重要的负面信号，说明战略有问题，但这时要做的是立刻找这批参与者要定性反馈，而不是放弃。(p.68-69)

7. **实验比传统战略规划快得多。** 整个实验几周就能完成，不到传统战略规划时间的十分之一，而且可以和规划同时进行。即使结果是负面的，也能影响战略。如果根本找不到计划中假设的那类员工，那就该 pivot 了（第 8 章详细讲）。(p.69)

8. **实验本身就是第一个产品。** 实验成功后，可以不断招募 early adopters、扩大迭代，最终做出正式产品。到产品大规模推出时，它已经有了现成的客户，解决了真实问题，产品规格也来自当下真实有效的反馈，而不是对未来的预测。(p.69-70)

9. **Kodak Gallery：先回答四个问题。** 产品副总裁 Mark Cook 要求团队在动手之前先回答：客户是否意识到自己有这个问题？如果有解决方案，他们会买吗？会从我们这里买吗？我们能做出来吗？常见的错误是直接跳到第四个问题。团队曾经把受欢迎的烫金婚礼贺卡改成节日贺卡，发布前几天才发现网站上看不出卡片的好看之处，生产也很难，等于把事情做反了。(p.70-71)

10. **Kodak Gallery 的 event album 实验。** 团队假设客户想创建活动相册，而且参加者会往朋友建的相册里上传照片。简陋的原型一上线，没有一个早期用户能成功创建相册，还有很多人抱怨缺功能。团队很沮丧，但 Cook 认为这并不失败：用户确实想要这个东西；被抱怨缺少的功能说明它们确实重要，没人提的功能也许没那么重要。beta 阶段通过 KISSinsights 发现用户想先调整照片顺序再邀请别人，于是 Cook 说服上级推迟营销发布。他强调，成功的标准是学会解决客户的问题，而不是交付某个功能。(p.71-72)

11. **Village Laundry Services（印度）。** 印度只有不到 7% 的人家里有洗衣机。前 P&G 品牌经理 Akshay Mehra 加入 VLS 后，第一个实验是在 Bangalore 街角的皮卡车上装一台家用洗衣机，成本不到 8,000 美元，衣服其实拿到别处洗，当天送回。他们发现客户愿意交出衣服，但担心卡车会带着衣服跑掉，于是改成更像售货亭的推车。后续迭代发现客户经常要熨烫，并愿意付双倍价格把 24 小时取件缩短到 4 小时。VLS 后来发展到三个城市 14 个网点，2010 年处理衣物 116,000 公斤（2009 年为 30,600 公斤），约六成业务来自回头客。(p.72-74)

12. **政府机构也能做 Lean Startup：CFPB。** 新成立的 CFPB 预算 5 亿美元，计划建呼叫中心接听公众投诉。作者建议把它当实验：核心假设是人们知道后会大量来电，但这仍只是假设。可以先用 Twilio 这类平台花几小时搭一个语音菜单热线，只在几个街区做定向宣传，总成本大约几千美元。这样能了解人们自己认为有什么问题、什么信息能促使他们打电话、实际来电比例是多少，并以此为基础逐步加入真人 caseworker。实际上 CFPB 已经按使用场景分批推出，先从信用卡开始。(p.74-77)

13. **计划只适用于稳定的环境。** 书中的这些管理者都很能干，也在按当下最佳实践做事，但他们需要改变"相信充分研究过的计划"这种思维。作者提醒，计划只有在长期稳定的运营历史下才管用，而今天的世界并没有越来越稳定。(p.77)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| "Just do it" school | 不设假设、"做出来看看会怎样"的创业方式，作者认为学不到东西 |
| Hypothesis | 对结果有明确预测、可以被检验和证伪的假设 |
| Value hypothesis | 检验产品在客户使用后是否真的提供价值 |
| Growth hypothesis | 检验新客户如何发现并开始使用产品 |
| Early adopters | 最迫切需要产品的客户，更能包容缺陷，更愿意反馈 |
| Concierge MVP | 为少数早期客户亲自提供定制化服务的 MVP（第 6 章） |
| Retention rate | 用户再次使用的比例，可以作为价值的代理指标 |
| Experiment as product | 成功的实验会自然成长为第一个产品和第一批客户 |

### 我的思考题

- 回想你做过的一个项目，它的 value hypothesis 和 growth hypothesis 分别是什么？当时有没有检验过？
- Zappos 和 VLS 都用"假装有完整业务"的方式做实验。这种做法在什么情况下可能伤害客户信任？
- Kodak Gallery 的团队把用户抱怨当作好消息。你如何区分"说明方向对了的抱怨"和"说明方向错了的抱怨"？

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Treat every startup effort as a scientific experiment: state a clear hypothesis, test it with a small, cheap product against real customer behavior, and catch mistakes early without giving up the long-term vision.

### Key Points

1. **The "just do it" school learns nothing.** Many teams plan to ship a product and see what happens. Ries notes that such a team is guaranteed to see what happens, but it will not necessarily gain validated learning. A core lesson of the scientific method is that if you cannot fail, you cannot learn. Without a prediction that could be proven wrong, there is no learning. (p.63)

2. **From alchemy to science.** The Lean Startup treats a startup's work as experiments that reveal which parts of the strategy are brilliant and which are crazy. A real experiment starts with a clear hypothesis that predicts an outcome, then tests it empirically. Scientific experiments are guided by theory. Startup experiments are guided by vision, and their goal is to learn how to build a sustainable business around it. (p.63-64)

3. **Zappos: think big, start small.** Founder Nick Swinmurn did not start with warehouses and distribution partners. He photographed shoes at local stores, posted the photos online, and when someone ordered a pair he went back and bought it at full price. The main question was whether people were ready to buy shoes online. Because he had to take payments, handle returns, and provide support, the experiment tested many other assumptions too. Compared with a survey, it gave three advantages: data on real behavior instead of hypothetical answers, direct contact with real customers and their needs, and room to be surprised by unexpected behavior such as returns. Amazon later acquired Zappos for a reported $1.2 billion. (p.64-65)

4. **HP's volunteer program is a startup too.** HP lets employees spend up to four hours a month of company time volunteering, yet most employees do not know about the policy, and most volunteering is manual labor that ignores their professional skills. Caroline Barlerin, who leads the effort, followed traditional practice with an 18-month road map and four years of impact metrics. So far she has only produced one-off wins and still does not know whether her vision can scale. Ries argues that the project faces extreme uncertainty and is full of untested assumptions, so it fits the Lean Startup approach. (p.65-67)

5. **Break the vision down into the value hypothesis and the growth hypothesis.** These are the two most important assumptions an entrepreneur makes:
   - **Value hypothesis**: does the product actually deliver value once people use it? Instead of asking employees how they feel, watch how many sign up to volunteer again and use retention as a proxy for value.
   - **Growth hypothesis**: how will new customers discover the product? If the program spreads virally, measure whether early participants actually recruit colleagues. (p.67-68)

6. **Start with early adopters and measure behavior, not opinions.** The experiment could use about a dozen long-time employees, chosen because they feel the sharpest gap between their daily work and the company's stated values. Early adopters need the product most, forgive mistakes more easily, and are eager to give feedback. A concierge MVP (Chapter 6) would give them the best possible experience, and then the team would track how many finish their assignments, volunteer again, and bring a colleague. If all ten decline to come back, that is a significant negative result pointing to a flawed strategy. The right response is to get qualitative feedback from those same participants right away, not to give up. (p.68-69)

7. **Experiments are much faster than strategic planning.** The whole experiment could run in a few weeks, less than a tenth of the time of traditional planning, and it can run alongside the planning process. Negative results still shape the strategy. If no employees with the assumed values conflict can be found, it is time to pivot (Chapter 8). (p.69)

8. **An experiment is also a first product.** A successful experiment lets the manager keep enlisting early adopters, widen each iteration, and eventually build the product. By the time it is ready for wide release, it already has customers, solves real problems, and has a specification grounded in what works today rather than guesses about tomorrow. (p.69-70)

9. **Kodak Gallery: answer four questions first.** Mark Cook, VP of products, asks his team to answer these before building: Do consumers recognize they have the problem? If there were a solution, would they buy it? Would they buy it from us? Can we build it? Teams usually jump straight to the fourth. Kodak Gallery once turned its popular gilded wedding cards into holiday cards, and only days before launch realized the cards looked unimpressive on the website and were hard to produce. Cook concluded they had done the work backward. (p.70-71)

10. **Kodak Gallery's event album experiment.** The team assumed customers would want to create event albums and that attendees would upload photos to albums made by friends. With a bare prototype, none of the early users managed to create an album, and many complained about missing features. The team was discouraged, but Cook saw real value: users clearly wanted the product, the missing features they complained about were proven important, and road map features nobody mentioned might matter less. During the beta, a KISSinsights survey showed users wanted to reorder photos before inviting others, so Cook persuaded his general manager to delay the marketing launch. For Cook, success means learning how to solve the customer's problem, and delivering a feature does not count by itself. (p.71-72)

11. **Village Laundry Services in India.** Fewer than 7 percent of people in India own a washing machine. Akshay Mehra, a former P&G brand manager, joined VLS, whose first experiment was a consumer washing machine mounted on a pickup truck on a Bangalore street corner. It cost under $8,000, and the laundry was actually cleaned off-site and returned the same day. Customers were happy to hand over clothes but worried the truck would drive off with them, so VLS switched to a kiosk-like cart. Later iterations showed that customers often wanted ironing and would pay double for four-hour instead of 24-hour service. VLS grew to 14 locations in three cities, handled 116,000 kg of laundry in 2010 versus 30,600 kg in 2009, and got about 60 percent of its business from repeat customers. (p.72-74)

12. **A lean startup in government: the CFPB.** The CFPB, a new federal agency created by the Dodd-Frank Act to protect consumers from predatory lending, had a $500 million budget and planned a call center for public complaints. Ries suggested treating it as an experiment. Its top assumption, that many people would call once they knew about it, was still only an assumption. The agency could set up a simple voice-menu hotline with a platform like Twilio in a few hours, advertise it only to a few city blocks, and spend a few thousand dollars. That would show what problems people think they have, what messages get them to call, and what share of people actually call, and it could grow step by step into a staffed service. The CFPB was in fact rolling out by use case, starting with credit cards. (p.74-77)

13. **Planning only works in stable conditions.** The managers in this book are capable and follow current best practices. Their challenge is to drop their faith in well-researched plans. Ries reminds readers that planning works only with a long, stable operating history, and the world is not becoming more stable. (p.77)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "Just do it" school | Shipping a product to "see what happens" without a hypothesis, which yields little learning |
| Hypothesis | A clear, testable prediction that could be proven false |
| Value hypothesis | Tests whether a product delivers value to customers once they use it |
| Growth hypothesis | Tests how new customers will discover and adopt the product |
| Early adopters | Customers who feel the need most acutely, forgive flaws, and give feedback eagerly |
| Concierge MVP | An MVP that gives a few early customers hands-on, personalized service (Chapter 6) |
| Retention rate | The share of users who come back, used as a proxy for value |
| Experiment as product | A successful experiment grows into the first product and first customer base |

### Discussion Questions

- Pick a project you have worked on. What were its value hypothesis and growth hypothesis, and did anyone test them?
- Zappos and VLS both ran experiments that looked like a full business from the outside. When could this approach damage customer trust?
- Kodak Gallery treated customer complaints as good news. How would you tell complaints that confirm your direction apart from complaints that show you are on the wrong path?
