## 中文版

### 一句话总结

作者借 Taylor 的 scientific management 一百周年回顾管理史，指出今天最大的浪费是高效地做错误的事，呼吁用科学方法研究创新，同时提醒 Lean Startup 不要重蹈 Taylorism 变成僵化教条的覆辙。

### 核心观点

1. **Taylor 发明了现代管理。** 1911 年出版的 *The Principles of Scientific Management* 带来了提升个人效率、management by exception（只关注意外的好坏结果）、把工作标准化成任务、task-plus-bonus 薪酬等做法。最核心的是：工作可以被研究，也可以被有意识地改进。作者认为管理本质上是对人组成的系统做工程设计。(p.262)

2. **Taylor 的革命"太成功"了。** Taylor 提倡的是把科学当作一种思维方式，但很多人只记住了他的具体技巧，比如时间动作研究、差别计件工资，甚至把工人当机器。这些做法造成了很大伤害。后来的 lean manufacturing 重新发现了一线工人的智慧，把效率的关注点从单个任务转向整个组织，但仍然保留了"工作可以用实验来改进"这个核心思想。(p.262-263)

3. **今天的问题是"该不该做"。** 20 世纪的创新主要是提高生产力，让人吃饱穿暖。现在我们几乎什么都造得出来，问题变成了一个东西该不该被造出来。Taylor 当年就说过，人的努力被浪费比物质浪费更严重，但因为看不见，很少有人在意。(p.263-264)

4. **最大的浪费是在错误的事情上很有效率。** 作者认为现代经济在生产上很高效，但浪费依然巨大，原因是大规模地做错误的事。他引用了 Drucker 的观点：把不该做的事做得很高效，是最没用的。他在各行各业都听到失败的发布、考虑不周的项目和大 batch 导致的恶性循环。很多人认为失败是必然的，或者觉得放慢速度、找到天才就能解决。作者认为这些都是 19 世纪的老办法。(p.264-265)

5. **Lean Startup 认为大部分浪费可以避免。** 光要求员工更努力没有用，现在的问题恰恰是在错误的事情上太努力。只盯着职能效率，就忘了创新真正的目标是弄清楚现在还不知道的东西。借用 Deming 的思路，重点不是定数字目标，而是改进达成目标的方法。(p.265)

6. **"组织超能力"的故事。** 一位参加过作者 workshop 的初级员工说，他在大公司里和 VP、GM 开会时，只要问几个简单问题，就能帮他们看出项目建立在可检验的假设上，并给出验证计划。领导们觉得他很聪明，他却很沮丧，因为他的洞察来自理论而不是天赋，而管理者得出的结论是要多找这样的聪明人，没有看到改变整个系统的机会。(p.265-266)

7. **"系统优先"的危险。** Taylor 那句系统优先的名言，完整原文其实说伟大的人仍然重要，好的系统首先要培养一流人才。但这一点很快被遗忘。结果带来两个问题：商业系统过于僵化，浪费了个人的创造力和智慧；过度强调计划、预防和流程，只适合相对静止的世界。工厂里的问题已经被 lean manufacturing 解决了，但新产品开发和创新仍在用过时的框架。作者希望 Lean Startup 不要掉进同样的简化陷阱，vision、敢冒险和勇气这些创业者的品质依然珍贵。(p.266-267)

8. **产品开发里的伪科学。** 作者认为今天管理创新者的方式缺乏科学严谨，可以称为 pseudoscience。例子包括：success theater，只挑支持愿景的数据；stealth mode，躲开所有客户反馈和外部问责；在总量指标图上标几个高亮点就声称有因果关系；用"我们学到了东西"来为失败开脱。真正的学习要在下一个循环里变成 validated learning。他也警告，不能让 pivot、MVP 这些概念变成新的伪科学。(p.268)

9. **我们这个时代的偏见是什么？** Taylor 有很多当时自称科学、现在看来是偏见的观点，比如认为贵族男性天生优于工人阶级、男性优于女性。作者反问：未来的人会看出我们有哪些偏见？他认为目前知道的只是冰山一角。Taylor 为了找到切割钢材的最佳方法，研究了二十五年以上，做了两万多次实验，经费全部来自生产力提升带来的利润。作者问，现在的知识工作管理者有谁会这样关心员工的工作方法？(p.269-270)

10. **新的研究计划。** 作者提议建立 startup testing labs，用小型 cross-functional 团队测试不同的开发方法。可以先用编程竞赛里有标准答案的题目做基准，再逐步提高不确定性，比如用真实客户问题让真实消费者评判，或者反复为同一问题做 MVP 比较转化率，也可以改变 cycle time。最重要的是找到清楚的方法让团队对 validated learning 负责，innovation accounting 只是其中一种。研究型大学和创业社区可以合作，大学能做的不止投资或办孵化器。(p.270-271)

11. **Long-Term Stock Exchange（LTSE）。** 公开市场的短期压力让大公司难以持续投入创新，部分原因是评价管理者的会计方法偏重 vanity metrics。作者提议建立一个新的交易所：上市公司除了季度利润，还要用 innovation accounting 报告内部创业情况，比如几年前还不存在的产品带来的收入；高管薪酬和长期表现挂钩；交易费用更高以减少日内交易；公司可以用更有利于长期投资的治理结构。(p.271-272)

12. **结语：停止浪费人的时间。** Lean Startup 要避免教条，科学本身是人类最有创造力的活动之一。如果一个组织里人人都有这种"超能力"，大家会明确说出假设并认真检验；不再在质量派和冒进派之间无休止争论，因为速度和质量都服务于客户的长期利益；面对失败会诚实地学习而不是互相指责；靠跳过不产生学习的工作来提速。作者用一句话收尾：最重要的是，我们会停止浪费人们的时间。(p.272-273)

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Marking a century since Taylor's scientific management, Ries argues that today's biggest waste comes from doing the wrong things efficiently, calls for applying the scientific method to innovation, and warns the Lean Startup not to harden into the kind of rigid doctrine Taylorism became.

### Key Points

1. **Taylor invented modern management.** *The Principles of Scientific Management*, published in 1911, introduced individual efficiency, management by exception (focusing only on unexpectedly good or bad results), standardized tasks, and task-plus-bonus pay. Its central idea was that work can be studied and deliberately improved. Ries describes management as engineering human systems. (p.262)

2. **Taylor's revolution was too successful.** Taylor meant science as a way of thinking, but many people remembered only his specific techniques, such as time and motion studies, differential piece rates, and treating workers like machines. These did real harm. Lean manufacturing later rediscovered the knowledge of frontline workers and shifted efficiency from the single task to the whole organization, while keeping the idea that work can be improved through experiments. (p.262-263)

3. **Today's question is whether something should be built.** Most innovation in the early twentieth century went into raising productivity to feed, clothe, and house people. Now we can build almost anything, so the question has become whether a thing should be built at all. Taylor himself wrote that wasted human effort costs more than wasted materials, yet gets little attention because it leaves nothing visible behind. (p.263-264)

4. **The biggest waste is efficiency on the wrong things.** Ries argues that the modern economy is efficient at making things but still hugely wasteful, because it works on the wrong things at industrial scale. He cites Drucker's point that doing efficiently what should not be done at all is useless. He hears about failed launches, ill-conceived projects, and large-batch death spirals in every industry. Many people think failure is inevitable, or that slowing down or finding gifted visionaries will fix it. Ries sees these as nineteenth-century answers. (p.264-265)

5. **The Lean Startup holds that most of this waste is preventable.** Telling people to work harder does not help. The problem is working too hard on the wrong things. Focusing on functional efficiency hides the real goal of innovation, which is learning what is not yet known. Following Deming, setting numerical targets matters less than fixing the method used to reach them. (p.265)

6. **The "organizational superpowers" story.** A junior employee from one of Ries's workshops found that in meetings with VPs and GMs at his large company, a few simple questions let him show that their projects rested on testable hypotheses and lay out a plan to validate them. Executives called him brilliant. He was frustrated, because his insight came from a theory rather than special talent, and managers concluded they needed more brilliant people. They missed the chance to change the system. (p.265-266)

7. **The dangers of putting the system first.** Taylor's full passage about the system coming first actually says great people are still needed and that a good system should develop first-class people. That part was quickly forgotten. Two problems followed: business systems became too rigid to use workers' creativity and judgment, and organizations leaned too hard on planning, prevention, and procedure, which only work in a fairly static world. Lean manufacturing fixed this on the factory floor, but new product development still uses an outdated framework. Ries hopes the Lean Startup avoids the same reductionism, since vision, bold risk-taking, and courage still matter. (p.266-267)

8. **Product development pseudoscience.** Ries says the way we manage innovators today lacks scientific rigor, and he calls it pseudoscience. Examples include success theater that picks only supporting data, stealth mode that avoids all customer feedback and accountability, highlighting points on a graph of gross metrics to claim cause and effect, and using "we learned a lot" to excuse failure. Real learning should show up as validated learning in the next cycle. He warns against building a new pseudoscience around pivots and MVPs. (p.268)

9. **What are our blind spots?** Taylor held views he called scientific that now look like prejudice, including the superiority of aristocratic men over the working class and of men over women. Ries asks what prejudices future generations will see in us, and says current knowledge is only the tip of the iceberg. Taylor spent more than twenty-five years and over twenty thousand experiments finding the best way to cut steel, paid for entirely by the productivity gains. Ries asks whether any modern knowledge-work manager cares that much about how employees work. (p.269-270)

10. **A new research program.** Ries proposes startup testing labs where small cross-functional teams try different development methods. They could start with programming competition problems that have clear answers and known solve times, then raise the uncertainty, for example by using real customer problems judged by real consumers, or by building MVPs for the same problem repeatedly to compare conversion rates. Cycle time could also be varied. Above all, we need clear ways to hold teams accountable for validated learning, and innovation accounting is only one option. Research universities could partner with entrepreneurial communities and do more than invest or run incubators. (p.270-271)

11. **The Long-Term Stock Exchange (LTSE).** Pressure from public markets for short-term results makes steady innovation hard for established companies, partly because managers are judged on vanity metrics. Ries proposes a new exchange where listed companies also report their internal entrepreneurship through innovation accounting, such as revenue from products that did not exist a few years earlier. Executive pay would track long-term performance, higher trading fees would discourage day trading, and companies could adopt governance that gives management room for long-term investment. (p.271-272)

12. **Conclusion: stop wasting people's time.** The movement must avoid doctrine, and science is one of humanity's most creative pursuits. In an organization where everyone had these superpowers, people would state assumptions openly and test them, stop arguing between quality defenders and reckless speed, treat speed and quality as serving the customer's long-term interest, respond to failure with honesty instead of blame, and go faster by skipping work that produces no learning. Ries closes by saying that above all, we would stop wasting people's time. (p.272-273)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scientific management | Taylor's theory that work can be studied and improved scientifically |
| Management by exception | Paying attention only to unexpectedly good or bad results |
| Success theater | Showing only the data that supports the vision to create an appearance of success |
| Pseudoscience | Practices that look scientific but lack rigorous testing |
| Startup testing labs | Ries's proposed labs for testing which product development methods work best |
| Long-Term Stock Exchange (LTSE) | Ries's proposed stock exchange designed to reward long-term thinking |

### Discussion Questions

- Can you think of a case where something that should not have been done was done very efficiently? Could it have been caught earlier?
- Ries warns that pivots and MVPs could become a new pseudoscience. When you hear people use these words, which uses skip real hypothesis testing?
- The junior employee in the story was treated as a genius instead of as evidence that the system could improve. In his place, how would you get managers to see the value of the method itself?
