## 中文版

### 一句话总结

MVP 是用最少的投入走完一轮 Build-Measure-Learn 的产品版本，目的是尽早开始学习。它可以是一段视频、一次手工服务或幕后由真人操作的系统，任何对学习没有直接帮助的工作都是浪费。

### 核心观点

1. **Groupon 从一个很简陋的 MVP 起步。** 创始人 Andrew Mason 原本做的是一个叫 The Point 的"集体行动平台"，效果不好，2008 年底决定尝试新方向。第一版 Groupon 就是一个换了皮的 WordPress 博客，每天发一篇帖子，coupon 用 FileMaker 生成 PDF 再用 Apple Mail 群发。第一单只有 20 个人在公司楼下餐厅买了买一送一的披萨。但这足以证明概念，Groupon 后来扩展到全球 375 个以上城市，有望成为史上最快达到 10 亿美元销售额的公司。(p.95-96)

2. **MVP 的定义。** MVP 不一定是能想到的最小产品，而是用最少的努力走完一轮 Build-Measure-Learn 的最快方式。传统产品开发追求完美、孵化期很长，MVP 的目标是开始学习，而不是结束学习。和原型或概念测试不同，MVP 要检验的是根本的商业假设，不只是设计或技术问题。(p.96)

3. **第一个产品不需要完美。** IMVU 融资时产品还有很多 bug，收入曲线虽然是曲棍球棒形，但顶点只有每月约 8,000 美元，投资人常问图表单位是不是"千"，他们只能回答"是个位"。但这些数据验证了两个 leap-of-faith assumptions：IMVU 在为客户创造价值，也有一个运转的 engine of growth。数字小是因为卖给的是 early adopters。(p.97)

4. **Early adopters 接受 80% 的方案。** 第一代 iPhone 没有复制粘贴、3G 和企业邮箱，早期用户照样排队购买；Google 早期只能很好地回答 Stanford、Linux 这类专门话题，也不妨碍早期用户称赞。early adopters 会用想象力补全产品的不足，他们在意的是率先使用。过于精致的产品反而让他们怀疑，所以超出他们需要的功能和打磨都是浪费。这对想做出"能给爸妈炫耀"的完美产品的创业者来说很难接受。(p.97-98)

5. **MVP 该多复杂需要判断，拿不准时就简化。** MVP 可以简单到只是一个广告（smoke test），也可以是有缺陷的早期原型。多数人都会高估 MVP 需要的功能。以一个提供一个月免费试用的服务为例，财务模型里某个单元格写着"10% 看到试用优惠的人会注册"，这其实就是一个 leap-of-faith 问题。先把产品做完再看客户反应是本末倒置：如果没人注册，所有功能都白做了。作者的结论是，任何超出开始学习所需的工作都是浪费，不管当时看起来多重要。(p.98-99)

6. **Video MVP：Dropbox。** Dropbox 的团队都是工程师，产品需要深度整合多个平台，很难做出能演示的原型。投资人觉得这个市场已经很拥挤、问题也不重要，但 Drew Houston 相信只要软件"像魔法一样好用"，客户就会涌来。于是他做了一段约三分钟的演示视频，针对技术 early adopters 社区，里面还藏了很多这个社区懂的梗。视频带来几十万访问，beta 等待名单一夜之间从 5,000 人涨到 75,000 人。这段视频就是 MVP，它用真实的注册行为验证了假设，而不是靠焦点小组或类比。(p.99-101)

7. **Concierge MVP：Food on the Table。** 这家 Austin 的公司根据家庭口味和本地超市的特价生成每周菜单和购物清单，完整版需要全国超市数据库、厨师和匹配算法。但它最开始只有一个客户、一家超市、零菜谱、零软件。CEO Manuel Rosso 和产品副总裁 Steve Sanderson 去超市和妈妈群做访谈，每次结束都尝试推销，大多被拒，终于有一位愿意订阅。之后每周 CEO 亲自上门送去定制的购物清单和菜谱，收集反馈，并收一张 9.95 美元的支票。(p.101-103)

8. **Concierge MVP 看似低效，实际进展很大。** 按传统标准，这完全不可扩展，也没有产品和像样的收入。但从 Lean Startup 的角度看，他们每周都在学习什么能让产品成功。客户多到忙不过来时才开始自动化：先改用邮件发送，再用软件自动解析特价，最后改为在线刷卡。开发团队始终在扩展已经有效的东西，所以浪费很少。作者强调这和小生意老板亲自服务客户不同：concierge MVP 是用来检验增长模型的学习活动，常见结果反而是推翻原有的增长模型，即使它本身赚钱。(p.103-104)

9. **Wizard of Oz MVP：Aardvark。** Max Ventilla 和 Damon Horowitz 想解决 Google 不擅长的主观问题，比如"球赛后在我所在的城市去哪喝一杯"。他们花了六个月搞清楚该做什么，方式是做一系列能用的产品原型给 beta 用户测试，而不是做市场研究。每个原型两到四周，邀请一两百个朋友试用，看有多少人回来，前五个结果都明确是负面的，Aardvark 是第六个。(p.104-106)

10. **幕后的八个人。** 选定 Aardvark 之后，他们仍然用真人在后台代替系统运行了九个月，雇了八个人处理问题、分类对话，甚至在系统自动化之前就完成了种子轮和 A 轮融资。遇到"该把问题转给社交网络里哪个人"这类难题，他们坚持不在早期解决，而是用 Wizard of Oz testing：客户以为自己在用真正的产品，其实是真人在背后操作。这种做法规模化后会亏钱，但在小规模上很容易搭建，并让他们能一次次 pivot。最终 Google 据报道以 5,000 万美元收购了 Aardvark。(p.107-108)

11. **先弄清客户是谁，才能定义质量。** 现代生产方法（Six Sigma、lean manufacturing、design thinking 等）都以高质量为原则，但前提是已经知道客户看重哪些属性，这对 startup 是个危险的假设。客户觉得 MVP 质量低，正好可以借机了解他们在乎什么。而很多著名产品一开始质量都很"低"，比如 Craigslist 早期的邮件通讯和 Groupon 的买一送一披萨。(p.108-109)

12. **IMVU 的 teleportation。** IMVU 早期的 avatar 不能移动，客户一致要求能走动。团队以为得花大钱做 The Sims 那样的流畅行走，于是先试了一个简单的办法：点哪里 avatar 就瞬间出现在哪里，连特效都没有。结果客户把"teleportation"列为最喜欢的前三项功能之一，还常说它比 The Sims 更先进。客户不在乎东西花了多少时间做，只在乎是否满足需求。(p.109-110)

13. **Lean Startup 不反对高质量，但质量要服务于赢得客户。** 创业者要愿意暂时放下传统的专业标准，尽早开始 validated learning，但这不等于马虎。会拖慢 Build-Measure-Learn 循环的缺陷本身就妨碍学习，不能容忍（Part Three 会讨论）。简单的规则是：去掉任何对你想要的学习没有直接贡献的功能、流程或工作。(p.110-111)

14. **做 MVP 的四个"减速带"。** 常见的顾虑有法律问题、担心竞争对手、品牌风险和对士气的影响。
   - **专利**：有些司法管辖区从产品公开发布起开始计算专利申请期限，发布 MVP 可能触发这个时钟，应该咨询法律顾问。(p.111-112)
   - **竞争对手偷点子**：这是作者听到最多的反对理由。他让创业者试着主动把一个次要的点子"送"给大公司的产品经理，结果发现大多数经理早已被好点子淹没，他们的难处在于优先级和执行。如果竞争对手知道点子后就能比你执行得更好，那保密也救不了你。唯一的胜法是比别人学得更快。(p.112)
   - **品牌**：可以换一个品牌名发布 MVP。startup 默默无闻、客户很少，正好可以低调实验，等产品被真实客户证明后再做公开营销。(p.112-113)
   - **坏消息和士气**：MVP 常带来坏消息。有远见的人尤其怕 false negative，也就是一个太小、有缺陷的 MVP 被客户拒绝。习惯 stage-gate 或 waterfall 模式的团队经常做 go/kill 决策，MVP 一失败就可能直接放弃项目。(p.113)

15. **事先承诺持续迭代，然后走向 innovation accounting。** 解决办法是事先约定，无论 MVP 测试结果如何都不放弃。成功的创业者既不会一遇到麻烦就放弃，也不会一路坚持到撞地，而是兼具坚持和灵活。多次迭代后若发现产品或战略的某部分有问题，就 pivot 到另一种实现愿景的方法。但外部投资人或公司 CFO 会问：没有达到承诺，是因为学到了关键东西，还是因为偷懒或方向错了？回答这个问题需要一套系统的方法，这就是 innovation accounting，第 7 章的主题。(p.113-114)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| Minimum viable product (MVP) | 用最少的努力走完一轮 Build-Measure-Learn 的产品版本，用来检验商业假设 |
| Early adopters | 愿意接受不完美产品、看重率先使用的早期客户 |
| Smoke test | 最简单的 MVP，几乎只是一则广告，用来测试需求 |
| Video MVP | 用演示视频代替真实产品来检验需求，如 Dropbox |
| Concierge MVP | 创始人亲自为极少数客户手工提供服务，以学习为目的，如 Food on the Table |
| Wizard of Oz testing | 客户以为在用真实产品，实际由真人在幕后操作，如 Aardvark |
| False negative | 客户拒绝了一个过小或有缺陷的 MVP，误导团队以为想法本身不行 |
| Go/kill decision | stage-gate 或 waterfall 模式中决定项目继续还是终止的节点 |
| Innovation accounting | 判断 startup 是否取得真实进展的系统方法（第 7 章） |

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

An MVP is the version of a product that completes one turn of the Build-Measure-Learn loop with the least effort, so learning can start early. It can be a video, a hand-delivered service, or a system secretly run by people, and any work that does not directly serve learning is waste.

### Key Points

1. **Groupon started with a very rough MVP.** Founder Andrew Mason first built The Point, a "collective activism platform," which disappointed. At the end of 2008 the team tried something new. The first Groupon was a reskinned WordPress blog with one post a day, and coupons were PDFs generated in FileMaker and sent through Apple Mail. The first deal sold two-for-one pizza to 20 people at a restaurant in the company's building. That was enough to prove the concept. Groupon grew to more than 375 cities and was on pace to be the fastest company ever to reach $1 billion in sales. (p.95-96)

2. **What an MVP is.** An MVP is not necessarily the smallest product imaginable. It is the fastest way through the Build-Measure-Learn loop with the least effort. Traditional development aims for perfection after a long incubation. An MVP aims to begin learning, not finish it. Unlike a prototype or concept test, it tests fundamental business hypotheses as well as design and technical questions. (p.96)

3. **First products are not meant to be perfect.** When IMVU raised money, its product was buggy, and its hockey-stick revenue curve topped out at about $8,000 a month. Investors asked whether the chart units were thousands, and the team had to say they were ones. Still, the numbers validated two leap-of-faith assumptions: IMVU created value for customers and had a working engine of growth. The totals were small because IMVU was selling to early adopters. (p.97)

4. **Early adopters accept an 80 percent solution.** Early adopters lined up for the first iPhone despite no copy and paste, no 3G, and no corporate e-mail. Google's first search engine handled niche topics like Stanford and Linux well, and early users praised it anyway. Early adopters fill in missing pieces with imagination and care most about being first. A product that is too polished makes them suspicious, so features and polish beyond what they need are waste. This is hard for founders who picture a slick product they can proudly show their parents. (p.97-98)

5. **How complex an MVP should be takes judgment, so simplify when unsure.** MVPs range from smoke tests that are little more than an ad to early prototypes with bugs and missing features. Most people overestimate the features an MVP needs. Take a service with a one-month free trial. Somewhere in the model a single cell says 10 percent of people who see the offer will sign up, and that is really a leap-of-faith question. Building the full product first and then checking reactions is backward, because if nobody signs up, all those features were wasted. Ries concludes that any work beyond what is needed to start learning is waste, however important it seemed. (p.98-99)

6. **The video MVP: Dropbox.** Dropbox's founders were engineers, and the product needed deep integration across many platforms, so a working prototype was hard to demo. Investors said the market was crowded and the problem unimportant, but Drew Houston believed customers would flock to software that worked like magic. He made a roughly three-minute demo video aimed at a tech early adopter community, filled with in-jokes that community would enjoy. It drove hundreds of thousands of people to the site, and the beta waiting list jumped from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight. The video was the MVP. It validated the assumption through actual sign-ups, without relying on a focus group or an analogy. (p.99-101)

7. **The concierge MVP: Food on the Table.** This Austin startup builds weekly meal plans and grocery lists from a family's tastes and local store sales. The full service needs a national store database, chefs, and matching algorithms. It started with one customer, one store, no recipes, and no software. CEO Manuel Rosso and VP of product Steve Sanderson interviewed people at supermarkets and moms' groups and tried to make a sale at the end of each interview. Most said no, until one person signed up. Every week the CEO visited her in person with a custom shopping list and recipes, collected feedback, and picked up a $9.95 check. (p.101-103)

8. **Inefficient on paper, real progress in practice.** By traditional standards this was unscalable, with no product and no meaningful revenue. Through the Lean Startup lens, the team learned more each week about what the product needed. Only when they were too busy to add customers did they automate: e-mail instead of home visits, software to parse sale lists, and online card payments instead of checks. Development always scaled something that already worked, so there was little waste. Ries separates this from a small business owner serving customers personally. A concierge MVP is a learning activity to test the growth model, and it often invalidates that model, even when the MVP itself is profitable. (p.103-104)

9. **The Wizard of Oz MVP: Aardvark.** Max Ventilla and Damon Horowitz wanted to answer subjective questions that Google handles poorly, such as where to get a drink after the ball game in your city. They spent six months figuring out what to build, not through market research but by building a series of working prototypes for beta testers. Each took two to four weeks, went to 100 to 200 friends, and was judged by how many came back. The results were clearly negative until the sixth prototype, which became Aardvark. (p.104-106)

10. **Eight people behind the curtain.** After choosing Aardvark, they kept humans running parts of the back end for nine months, hiring eight people to handle queries and classify conversations, and raised their seed and Series A rounds before the system was automated. For hard problems like routing each question to the right person in a user's network, they refused to solve them early and used Wizard of Oz testing instead: customers believe they are using the real product while humans do the work behind the scenes. That would lose money at scale but is easy to build small, and it let the team pivot again and again. Google later acquired Aardvark for a reported $50 million. (p.107-108)

11. **Quality cannot be defined until you know the customer.** Modern methods such as Six Sigma, lean manufacturing, and design thinking treat quality as a first principle, but they assume the company already knows what customers value. In a startup that is a risky assumption. If customers see an MVP as low quality, that is a chance to learn what they care about. And many famous products launched in a "low-quality" state and were loved anyway, such as Craigslist's early e-mail newsletter and Groupon's two-for-one pizza. (p.108-109)

12. **IMVU's teleportation.** IMVU's early avatars could not move, and customers consistently asked for movement. The team feared it would need expensive Sims-style walking, so it tried a cheap hack first: click a spot and the avatar instantly appears there, with no animation or sound. Customers then listed "teleportation" among their top three favorite features and often called it more advanced than The Sims. Customers do not care how long something took to build. They care whether it meets their needs. (p.109-110)

13. **Lean Startup is not against quality, but quality should serve winning customers.** Founders must be willing to set aside traditional professional standards to start validated learning early, but that does not mean working sloppily. Defects that slow down the Build-Measure-Learn loop interfere with learning and should not be tolerated (Part Three covers this). The simple rule is to remove any feature, process, or effort that does not directly contribute to the learning you want. (p.110-111)

14. **Four speed bumps in building an MVP.** The common concerns are legal issues, competitors, branding, and morale.
   - **Patents**: in some jurisdictions the patent filing window starts when a product goes public, and releasing an MVP may start that clock. Get legal advice. (p.111-112)
   - **Competitors stealing the idea**: this is the objection Ries hears most. He challenges founders to try to get a product manager at a big company to steal one of their lesser ideas. Most managers are already swamped with good ideas, and their real struggle is prioritization and execution. If a competitor can outexecute you once it knows the idea, secrecy will not save you. The only way to win is to learn faster than anyone else. (p.112)
   - **Branding**: launch the MVP under a different brand name. Startups are obscure and have few customers, so they can experiment quietly and do a public launch once real customers have proven the product. (p.112-113)
   - **Bad news and morale**: MVPs often bring bad news. Visionaries fear a false negative, where customers reject an MVP that is too small or flawed. Teams used to stage-gate or waterfall methods make regular go/kill decisions and may abandon a project after one failed MVP. (p.113)

15. **Commit to iteration in advance, then move to innovation accounting.** The fix is to agree ahead of time that the team will not give up hope, whatever the MVP shows. Successful entrepreneurs neither quit at the first sign of trouble nor persevere all the way into the ground. They combine perseverance with flexibility. After many iterations, if part of the product or strategy proves flawed, they pivot to a different way of reaching the vision. Investors and corporate CFOs will still ask whether missed promises reflect real learning or just slacking or bad direction. Answering that requires a disciplined system, innovation accounting, which is the subject of Chapter 7. (p.113-114)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Minimum viable product (MVP) | The version of a product that completes one Build-Measure-Learn loop with the least effort, used to test business hypotheses |
| Early adopters | Early customers who accept imperfect products and value being first |
| Smoke test | The simplest MVP, little more than an ad, used to test demand |
| Video MVP | A demo video that stands in for the real product to test demand, as at Dropbox |
| Concierge MVP | Founders personally serve a handful of customers by hand to learn, as at Food on the Table |
| Wizard of Oz testing | Customers think they use a real product while people do the work behind the scenes, as at Aardvark |
| False negative | Customers reject a too-small or flawed MVP, misleading the team into thinking the idea itself failed |
| Go/kill decision | A checkpoint in stage-gate or waterfall development where a project is continued or stopped |
| Innovation accounting | A systematic way to judge whether a startup is making real progress (Chapter 7) |

### Discussion Questions

- IMVU's teleportation shows that "low quality" depends on the customer. Have you seen a feature the team thought was crude but users loved?
- Wizard of Oz testing lets customers believe they are using an automated system. Where is the ethical line for this approach?
