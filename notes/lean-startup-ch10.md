## 中文版

### 一句话总结

可持续增长来自老客户带来新客户，startup 应该找出自己靠的是哪一种 engine of growth（sticky、viral 或 paid），然后只盯住那台发动机对应的少数关键指标。

### 核心观点

1. **两家完全不同的公司，同一个问题。** 一家做收藏品交易平台，一家卖企业级数据库软件。两家都有早期客户和收入，也都融到了钱，但增长都停滞了。CEO 们想知道该加大广告、改进产品，还是调整定价。作者发现它们用的是同一种 engine of growth，所以困惑也一样。(p.200-201)

2. **可持续增长的规则：新客户来自老客户的行为。** 作者把一次性的广告或公关噱头排除在外。老客户带来增长有四种方式：口碑；产品使用本身的副作用（例如用 PayPal 转账时对方自然接触到 PayPal）；用收入而非融资来支付的广告，只要获客成本低于客户带来的收入；重复购买或使用（订阅、日用品）。这些来源驱动的反馈循环就是 engine of growth，循环转得越快，公司长得越快。(p.201-202)

3. **Engine 帮 startup 聚焦。** 产品上线后，团队很容易花大量时间争论优先级：找新客户、服务老客户、提升质量，还是降成本。大多数改进想法只是边际优化。Engine of growth 框架给出一小组真正重要的指标，让团队专注于能带来 validated learning 的大实验。(p.203)

4. **Sticky engine：看 churn。** 两家开头的公司都靠留住客户长期使用：收藏者会反复回来看新货，数据库客户一旦在上面搭好系统就很难换（lock-in）。这类公司要紧盯 churn rate，即一段时间内不再使用产品的客户比例。规则很简单：新客户获取率高于 churn rate，产品就增长。增长速度叫 *rate of compounding*，等于自然增长率减去 churn rate。(p.203-204)

5. **一家公司的真实数字。** 这两家公司原来看的是客户总数等通用指标。其中一家按 sticky 模型重新分析后发现：留存率 61%，新客户增长率 39%，两者几乎抵消，复合增长率只有 0.02%。好消息是新客户其实一直在来，解决办法是让现有客户更投入，比如增加更多更好的商品、推送限时优惠。这和"没增长就多花钱做营销"的直觉正好相反。PointCast 当年也有同样的问题。(p.204-205)

6. **Viral engine：看 viral coefficient。** Viral 增长和普通口碑不同，传播是正常使用产品的必然结果，客户并不是刻意推荐。Hotmail 在每封邮件底部加了一行免费注册邮箱的链接，六个月内用户超过 100 万，18 个月时 1,200 万用户，以 4 亿美元卖给 Microsoft。Tupperware 的家庭聚会也是这种模式。Viral coefficient 衡量每个新客户平均带来几个新客户：0.1 时增长很快就停，大于 1.0 时指数增长。(p.205-207)

7. **Viral 产品常常不直接收费。** 因为任何注册和邀请过程中的阻力都会伤害增长，很多 viral 产品靠广告等间接收入。这让检验 value hypothesis 更难。价值交换可以是金钱（Tupperware），也可以是时间和注意力（Facebook）。卖广告的公司实际上服务两类客户：用户和广告主。(p.208)

8. **Paid engine：看 LTV 和 CPA 的差。** 一家公司每个客户赚 1 美元、获客花 0.8 美元，另一家每个客户赚 10 万美元、获客花 8 万美元，两者增长速度一样，因为都有 20% 可以再投入获客。每个客户在整个生命周期内扣除可变成本后的收入叫 customer lifetime value (LTV)；每获得一个客户的成本叫 cost per acquisition (CPA)。LTV 高于 CPA 就能增长，二者的差（marginal profit）决定增长速度。靠融资或噱头填补亏损不可持续，很多 dot-com 公司就这样倒下。(p.208-210)

9. **IMVU 的 engine of growth pivot。** IMVU 原本以为作为 IM 插件可以 viral 传播，但用户想用它结识新朋友，不想拉老朋友进来，觉得拉新是公司的事。好在用户愿意付的钱多于广告获客成本，IMVU 转为靠 paid engine 增长。(p.209)

10. **Paid engine 也包括销售团队和门店。** 外呼销售团队、依赖客流的零售店，也都属于 paid engine，这些成本都应计入 CPA。一家做团队协作工具的 startup 从服务业余爱好者转向服务大型机构和 NGO，却没换增长引擎。一个大 NGO 想买，却因为几百美元一个月的价格太便宜而没有采购流程，也需要 startup 提供不了的实施支持。后来这家公司组建了外呼销售团队，每个客户的收入从几美元涨到几万甚至几十万美元。(p.210-211)

11. **获客渠道的价格会被竞争抬高。** 好的店面和面向富裕人群的广告更贵，因为竞争者都能从这些客户身上赚更多。长期来看，想靠 paid engine 增长，必须在某类客户身上有与众不同的变现能力。IMVU 的用户里有很多青少年、低收入成人和海外用户，别人觉得他们不会付费，IMVU 开发了手机账单付款、邮寄现金等方式，因此能出更高的获客价格。(p.211)

12. **一次专注一台发动机。** 技术上一个产品可以同时有多种增长引擎，但作者的经验是成功的 startup 通常只专注一个。同时追踪三种的仪表盘会造成混乱。只有彻底尝试过一种之后，才考虑 pivot 到另一种。(p.212)

13. **用 engine 让 product/market fit 更具体。** Marc Andreessen 提出 product/market fit，指找到大量认同产品的客户的时刻。作者说，如果你还在问自己有没有达到，那就是还没有。他反对两种隐含的理解：pivot 等于失败，达到 fit 后就不用再 pivot。每种 engine 都能量化，例如 viral coefficient 达到 0.9 以上就接近成功。结合 innovation accounting，viral 公司可以专注影响 viral loop 的开发，不必急着建营销和销售；paid 公司则必须尽快建立这些职能。(p.212-213)

14. **看方向，不看绝对数。** 两家 sticky 公司，A 的复合增长率半年内从 0.1% 升到 5.0%，B 一直在 10% 左右徘徊。虽然 B 现在长得更快，但 A 在真正进步，B 停滞不前。(p.214)

15. **每台发动机最终都会熄火。** 每种引擎都依赖特定的一群客户及其习惯和渠道，这群人迟早会用完。MVP 只针对 early adopters，转向主流客户需要大量额外工作。危险在于，增长放缓可能要几个月甚至几年才出现，用 vanity metrics 的公司会误以为产品在变好，其实增长全来自已有的引擎。大公司也面临同样的问题，所以需要一边调优现有引擎，一边培育新的增长来源（第 12 章），而这需要 adaptive organization（第 11 章）。(p.214-215)

### 三种增长引擎对比

| Engine | 增长机制 | 关键指标 | 增长条件 | 书中例子 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sticky | 留住客户长期、重复使用 | Churn rate、rate of compounding | 新客户获取率 > churn rate | 收藏品平台、企业数据库、手机运营商 |
| Viral | 使用产品时自然传播给他人 | Viral coefficient | Viral coefficient > 1.0 | Hotmail、Tupperware、Facebook |
| Paid | 用客户带来的收入购买新客户 | LTV、CPA | LTV > CPA | IMVU、外呼销售型企业软件 |

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Sustainable growth comes from past customers bringing in new ones, and a startup should identify which engine of growth it relies on (sticky, viral, or paid) and focus on the few metrics that drive that engine.

### Key Points

1. **Two very different startups, one problem.** One is building a marketplace for collectibles traders. The other sells database software to large enterprises. Both had early customers, revenue, and outside funding, and both saw growth flatline. Their CEOs asked whether to spend more on marketing, improve the product, or change pricing. Ries found both were running on the same engine of growth, which explained their shared confusion. (p.200-201)

2. **The rule of sustainable growth: new customers come from the actions of past customers.** One-time ads and publicity stunts do not count. Past customers drive growth in four ways: word of mouth; side effects of product use (a PayPal payment exposes the recipient to PayPal); advertising paid for from revenue, as long as acquisition cost is below customer revenue; and repeat purchase or use (subscriptions, groceries). These sources power feedback loops called engines of growth, and the faster the loop turns, the faster the company grows. (p.201-202)

3. **Engines create focus.** Once a product is on the market, teams can burn huge amounts of time debating whether to find new customers, serve current ones, improve quality, or cut costs. Most improvement ideas are marginal optimizations. The engines of growth framework narrows attention to a small set of metrics and to the big experiments that produce validated learning. (p.203)

4. **The sticky engine: watch churn.** Both opening startups depend on long-term retention. Collectors keep checking for new items, and database customers are locked in once they build on the product. Sticky companies track churn rate, the share of customers in a period who stop engaging. The rule is simple: if new customer acquisition exceeds churn, the product grows. Growth speed is the *rate of compounding*, which equals the natural growth rate minus the churn rate. (p.203-204)

5. **What the numbers showed.** Both startups had been tracking generic indicators like total customers. One remodeled its business on the sticky template and found 61 percent retention and a 39 percent new customer growth rate. The two nearly canceled out, leaving a compounding rate of just 0.02 percent. The good news was that plenty of new customers were arriving. The fix was to make the product more engaging for existing customers, for example with more and better listings or alerts about limited-time sales. This runs against the instinct to spend more on marketing when growth stalls. PointCast had suffered the same problem. (p.204-205)

6. **The viral engine: watch the viral coefficient.** Viral growth differs from word of mouth because spreading is a necessary result of normal use, and customers are not trying to promote anything. Hotmail added a line with a free sign-up link to the bottom of every email. Within six months it passed 1 million users, and at eighteen months, with 12 million subscribers, it sold to Microsoft for $400 million. Tupperware parties follow the same logic. The viral coefficient measures how many new customers each new customer brings. At 0.1 the loop fizzles out, and above 1.0 growth is exponential. (p.205-207)

7. **Viral products often do not charge users.** Any friction in signing up or inviting friends hurts growth, so many viral products earn money indirectly through advertising. That makes testing the value hypothesis harder. The value exchange can be money (Tupperware) or time and attention (Facebook). Ad-supported companies serve two customer groups, consumers and advertisers. (p.208)

8. **The paid engine: watch LTV minus CPA.** A company that earns $1 per customer and pays 80 cents to acquire each one grows at the same rate as a company earning $100,000 per customer and spending $80,000 to acquire each, because both can reinvest 20 percent. Revenue per customer over their lifetime, minus variable costs, is customer lifetime value (LTV). The cost of acquiring one customer is cost per acquisition (CPA). If LTV exceeds CPA, the product grows, and the gap (marginal profit) sets the speed. Covering losses with investor money or stunts is not sustainable, and many dot-com failures learned this. (p.208-210)

9. **IMVU's engine of growth pivot.** IMVU expected its IM add-on to spread virally, but customers wanted to use it to meet new people, not to bring in existing friends, and they saw recruiting as the company's job. Because customers paid more than it cost to reach them with ads, IMVU grew through the paid engine instead. (p.209)

10. **The paid engine goes beyond ads.** Outbound sales forces and retail stores that depend on foot traffic also run on the paid engine, and those costs belong in CPA. One startup making collaboration tools pivoted from hobbyists and small clubs to large enterprises and NGOs without changing its engine. A major NGO wanted to buy but had no process for purchasing something that cost only a few hundred dollars a month, and it needed rollout support the startup could not provide. After building an outbound sales team, the company went from a few dollars per customer to tens and then hundreds of thousands of dollars. (p.210-211)

11. **Competition bids up acquisition costs.** Prime storefronts and ads aimed at wealthy customers cost more because competitors earn more from those customers. Over time, every acquisition channel's CPA rises. Long-term growth on the paid engine requires a differentiated ability to monetize some group of customers. IMVU's users included many teens, low-income adults, and international customers whom others assumed would not pay. IMVU let them pay through mobile phone bills or cash by mail, so it could afford to pay more to acquire them. (p.211)

12. **Focus on one engine at a time.** A product can technically run more than one engine, but in Ries's experience successful startups specialize in one. Dashboards that track all three create confusion. A startup should pursue one engine thoroughly before considering a pivot to another. (p.212)

13. **Engines make product/market fit measurable.** Marc Andreessen coined product/market fit for the moment a startup finds a large set of customers who resonate with its product. Ries says that if you have to ask whether you have it, you do not. He rejects two common readings: that a pivot means failure, and that reaching fit ends the need to pivot. Each engine can be defined quantitatively, so, for example, a viral coefficient of 0.9 or more signals a startup is close. Combined with innovation accounting, a viral startup can focus on work that affects the viral loop and skip building marketing and sales, while a paid startup must build those functions urgently. (p.212-213)

14. **Direction matters more than raw numbers.** Two sticky startups: company A's compounding growth rate climbed from 0.1 percent to 5.0 percent over six months, while company B hovered around 10 percent. B is growing faster right now, but A is making real progress and B is stuck. (p.214)

15. **Every engine eventually runs out of gas.** Each engine is tied to a particular set of customers, habits, and channels, and that set will be exhausted. An MVP built for early adopters needs a lot of extra work to reach mainstream customers. The slowdown may take months or years to appear, so companies using vanity metrics can believe the product is improving when all the growth comes from an engine that is already working. Established companies face the same risk. They must tune the current engine while developing new sources of growth (Chapter 12), which requires an adaptive organization (Chapter 11). (p.214-215)

### The Three Engines of Growth

| Engine | How it grows | Key metrics | Growth condition | Examples from the book |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sticky | Retaining customers for long-term, repeat use | Churn rate, rate of compounding | New customer acquisition > churn rate | Collectibles marketplace, enterprise database, mobile carriers |
| Viral | Spreading to others as a side effect of normal use | Viral coefficient | Viral coefficient > 1.0 | Hotmail, Tupperware, Facebook |
| Paid | Reinvesting customer revenue to buy new customers | LTV, CPA | LTV > CPA | IMVU, enterprise software with outbound sales |

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Engine of growth | The feedback loop a startup uses to achieve sustainable growth |
| Sustainable growth | Growth where new customers come from the actions of past customers, excluding one-time activities |
| Churn rate | The share of customers in a period who stop engaging with the product |
| Rate of compounding | Natural growth rate minus churn rate |
| Viral coefficient | The average number of new customers each new customer brings in |
| Viral loop | The feedback loop that powers the viral engine |
| Customer lifetime value (LTV) | Revenue from a customer over their lifetime, minus variable costs |
| Cost per acquisition (CPA) | The cost of acquiring one new customer |
| Marginal profit | The gap between LTV and CPA, which sets how fast the paid engine turns |
| Product/market fit | Marc Andreessen's term for finding a large set of customers who resonate with the product |
