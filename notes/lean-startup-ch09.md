## 中文版

### Part Three 导读

Part Three 讨论的是 startup 在开始增长之后怎样保持速度。作者先抛出几个没有标准答案的问题：产品多久发布一次、在还没成功时要为基础设施投入多少、员工每天该做什么、怎样让整个组织为学习负责。他认为 lean manufacturing 在工厂里遇到过类似问题，关键是先分清哪些活动创造价值、哪些是浪费。对 startup 来说，价值就是 validated learning。(p.177-178)

作者反对"公司大了就必然官僚"的说法，认为打好基础的 Lean Startup 可以长成仍然灵活的 lean enterprise。四章的分工是：第 9 章讲 small batches 和 just-in-time scalability；第 10 章讲三种 engine of growth（paid、viral、sticky）及各自的指标；第 11 章讲怎样用 Five Whys 等工具建立 adaptive organization；第 12 章讲成熟公司怎样同时管理持续性创新和颠覆式创新。书的最后还有一个尾声 "Waste Not"。(p.178-179)

### 一句话总结

一次只做一小批、尽快走完整个流程，看起来效率低，实际上能更早发现问题、减少返工，让 startup 比对手更快地走完 Build-Measure-Learn 循环。

### 核心观点

1. **装信封的实验。** 《Lean Thinking》里讲过一个故事：两个女儿认为应该先把所有信纸叠好，再统一贴封条、贴邮票；父亲坚持一个信封一个信封地完成。结果父亲更快。一次一件的做法在 lean manufacturing 里叫 *single-piece flow*，batch size 为 1。(p.180)

2. **小批量为什么更快。** 大批量做法忽略了整理、堆放、搬运半成品所花的时间。更重要的是，如果信纸塞不进信封，或者信封封不上，小批量几乎马上就能发现，大批量要到最后才发现，还得全部返工。放到公司里，最大的问题是：如果客户根本不想要这个产品，哪种流程能更早告诉你？(p.181)

3. **Toyota 靠小批量赢了大规模生产。** 战后日本市场小、资本少，Toyota 用不起美国那种专门化的大机器，只能用通用机器小批量生产。Shigeo Shingo 发明了 SMED（Single-Minute Exchange of Die），通过重新设计工作流程，把原本要几个小时的换模时间压到十分钟以内。小批量让 Toyota 能生产更多样的产品，逐步进入更大的市场，2008 年成为全球最大车企。(p.181-183)

4. **Andon cord：发现问题就停线。** 小批量最大的好处是质量问题暴露得早。Toyota 的任何工人发现缺陷都可以拉 andon cord 求助，必要时停下整条生产线。这看上去打断了流程，但早发现、早修复的收益更大。在 startup 里，目标是尽快学会怎样建立可持续的业务，小批量能把最终白费的时间、金钱和精力降到最低。(p.183)

5. **IMVU 的 continuous deployment。** 传统产品开发像一条虚拟流水线：产品经理、设计、工程、测试逐级交接，所有改动打包成一个大版本发布（作者举了 iPhone 4 有 1,500 多项改动的例子）。IMVU 让工程师和设计师一起一次做一个功能，做完就发布给一小部分用户看效果，平均每天大约改动 50 次。(p.184-185)

6. **产品的 immune system。** 要这样快速发布，必须马上检查缺陷。IMVU 有大量自动化测试，还持续监控业务指标本身。作者举例：如果有人把结账按钮改成白底白字，功能测试会通过，但顾客买不了东西。IMVU 的系统能发现这种业务后果，然后自动撤回有问题的改动、通知团队、禁止继续提交，直到找到并修复根本原因（第 11 章展开讲）。Wealthfront 在 SEC 监管环境下也做到了每天十几次发布。(p.185-186)

7. **软件之外也能用。** 三股力量让其他行业也能快速迭代：硬件的价值越来越由软件决定；lean 生产线可以快速换型，没有旧版本库存拖累；3D 打印和快速原型工具让创业者也能低成本做出小批量实物。要点不在于每天发布 50 次，而在于缩小 batch size，比对手更快走完 Build-Measure-Learn 循环。(p.186-188)

8. **SGW Designworks 的例子。** 这家位于 Boise 的公司为军方客户设计一套野外 X 光系统的支架。先用 3D CAD 做虚拟原型，三天后交付 CNC 加工的铝制实物原型，客户拿去给军方评审，再用五天完成一轮迭代。项目启动三周半后，第一批 40 台就可以交付。SGW 用同样方法在 12 个月里做了 8 个产品。(p.188-189)

9. **教育里的小批量。** 数学老师的课程一年最多只能换一次，这是典型的大批量系统。School of One 给每个学生一份每日学习 "playlist"，每个活动都内嵌评估。老师改了教学顺序，可以马上看到正学到这里的学生的反应，效果好就推给所有人。(p.190-191)

10. **Large-batch death spiral。** 从个人效率看，设计师一口气画完 30 张图再交给工程很合理。但工程一有疑问就会打断设计师，返工也随之而来。作者发现在大批量公司里，产品经理和设计师每个版本要返工五六次。因为每次推进都带来额外开销，大家倾向于把批量做得更大，而软件没有物理上限，最后变成"赌上公司"的大版本，谁都不敢发布。作者待过的一家公司就因此陷入危机并更换了管理层。医院药房和抽血送检也有同样的问题，改成小批量后总成本反而更低。(p.191-193)

11. **Pull, don't push。** 修车时用掉一个保险杠，经销商库存出现一个"空缺"，信号依次传到 Toyota 的零件配送中心、区域仓库和工厂，工厂就再做一个。这就是 just-in-time。采用后，work-in-progress (WIP) 库存大幅减少。startup 的 WIP 看不见：未完成的设计、未验证的假设、大部分 business plan 都是 WIP。(p.193-195)

12. **拉动工作的是假设，不是客户。** 有人把 Lean Startup 误解成"客户要什么就做什么"，但客户常常不知道自己想要什么。Build-Measure-Learn 的执行顺序是 Build 在前，规划顺序却是反过来的：先想清楚要学什么，再倒推需要做什么产品实验。拉动产品开发的是关于客户的假设，其他工作都是浪费。(p.195-196)

13. **Alphabet Energy 的例子。** 这家做废热发电（thermoelectric）的公司选择基于硅片做材料，可以借用现成的半导体产业设施，从概念到实物只要六周。相比之下，SunPower 要自建工厂，BrightSource 融资 2.91 亿美元建电站。Alphabet 最初假设电厂燃气轮机是理想市场，但电力公司风险容忍度低，三个月后就 pivot 了，经过多次 customer segment pivot，转向可以在厂区局部试用新技术的制造企业。到当时为止它只融了大约 100 万美元。(p.196-198)

14. **流程是文化的基础。** Toyota 最了不起的是建成了一个学习型组织。Lean 技术只是一个高效组织的外在表现，流程是基础，没有它，鼓励学习和创新的努力都会落空。Part Three 剩下的部分讨论这种新工作方式带来的人的挑战。(p.198)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| Batch size | 每次从一个工序流向下一个工序的工作量 |
| Single-piece flow | batch size 为 1，一次完整做完一件 |
| SMED | Single-Minute Exchange of Die，Shigeo Shingo 提出的快速换模方法 |
| Andon cord | Toyota 生产线上的拉绳，任何人发现问题都可以停线 |
| Continuous deployment | 每个小改动完成后立即自动发布，并配合自动检测和回滚 |
| Immune system | IMVU 用来自动发现功能和业务问题并撤回改动的防御机制 |
| Large-batch death spiral | 批量越做越大、越来越不敢发布的恶性循环 |
| Pull / Just-in-time | 下游需要时才向上游要货，而不是提前堆库存 |
| WIP inventory | 在制品库存；在 startup 里指未验证的假设、未完成的设计等 |
| Hypothesis pull | 由要检验的假设来决定做什么，而不是由客户需求直接决定 |

### 我的思考题

- 你的课程项目或实习中，有没有"大批量"的做法？比如写完整份报告才第一次给别人看。
- 你所在的团队有没有类似 andon cord 的机制，让任何人都能叫停有问题的工作？
- 假设驱动的 pull 和"听客户的"有什么区别？在什么情况下两者会得出不同的开发计划？

## English Version

### Part Three Overview

Part Three is about how a startup keeps its speed once it starts to grow. Ries opens with questions that have no obvious answers: how often to release, how much to invest in infrastructure before success arrives, what employees should spend their days on, and how to hold a whole organization accountable for learning. Lean manufacturing faced similar questions on the factory floor, and its first step was to separate value-creating work from waste. For a startup, value means validated learning. (p.177-178)

Ries rejects the idea that bureaucracy is the inevitable fate of a maturing company. With the right foundation, a Lean Startup can become a lean enterprise that stays agile. The chapters divide the work this way: Chapter 9 covers small batches and just-in-time scalability, Chapter 10 covers the three engines of growth (paid, viral, sticky) and their metrics, Chapter 11 covers building an adaptive organization with tools like the Five Whys, and Chapter 12 covers how established companies manage sustaining and disruptive innovation together. An epilogue called "Waste Not" closes the book. (p.178-179)

### One-Sentence Summary

Doing work in small batches looks inefficient, but it surfaces problems earlier, cuts rework, and lets a startup move through the Build-Measure-Learn loop faster than its competitors.

### Key Points

1. **The envelope experiment.** In *Lean Thinking*, two young daughters argue that newsletters should be folded all at once, then sealed, then stamped. Their father finishes each envelope one at a time and wins the race. Lean manufacturing calls this *single-piece flow*, a batch size of one. (p.180)

2. **Why small batches are faster.** The large-batch approach ignores the time spent sorting, stacking, and moving piles of half-finished work. More importantly, if the letters do not fit or the envelopes will not seal, small batches reveal it right away, while large batches reveal it at the end and force full rework. For a company, the key question is which process tells you sooner that customers do not want the product. (p.181)

3. **Toyota beat mass production with small batches.** Postwar Japan had a small market and little capital, so Toyota could not buy huge specialized machines. It used general-purpose machines and small batches instead. Shigeo Shingo created SMED (Single-Minute Exchange of Die) and cut changeover times from hours to under ten minutes by redesigning the work itself. Small batches let Toyota make a wider variety of products, move into larger markets, and become the world's largest automaker in 2008. (p.181-183)

4. **The andon cord.** The biggest benefit of small batches is catching quality problems early. Any Toyota worker can pull the andon cord to get help and, if needed, stop the whole line. The interruptions cost less than the benefit of fixing defects fast. In a startup, the goal is learning how to build a sustainable business, and small batches minimize the time, money, and effort that end up wasted. (p.183)

5. **Continuous deployment at IMVU.** Traditional product development is a virtual assembly line of handoffs from product managers to designers to engineers to testers, with all changes bundled into one big release (Ries cites the more than 1,500 changes in the iPhone 4). At IMVU, engineers and designers worked together on one feature at a time and shipped it to a small group of users as soon as it was ready. IMVU averages about fifty product changes a day. (p.184-185)

6. **The product's immune system.** Shipping this fast requires checking for defects immediately. IMVU had extensive automated tests and also monitored business health. Ries's example: if someone turns the checkout button white on a white background, functional tests still pass, yet nobody can buy anything. IMVU's system detects business consequences like this, then automatically removes the change, alerts the team, and blocks further changes until the root cause is fixed (covered in Chapter 11). Wealthfront runs more than a dozen releases a day in an SEC-regulated environment. (p.185-186)

7. **Beyond software.** Three trends bring rapid iteration to other industries: hardware whose value increasingly comes from software, production lines built for quick changeovers with no leftover inventory, and 3D printing and rapid prototyping that let entrepreneurs make small, high-quality batches cheaply. The lesson is to shrink batch size so you get through Build-Measure-Learn faster than competitors, whether or not you ship fifty times a day. (p.186-188)

8. **SGW Designworks.** This Boise firm designed the support frame for a field x-ray system for a military customer. It started with 3D CAD models, delivered CNC-machined aluminum prototypes three days later, and completed another full design cycle in five days. The first run of forty units was ready three and a half weeks after the project began. SGW used the same process to deliver eight products in twelve months. (p.188-189)

9. **Small batches in education.** A math teacher can change the curriculum at most once a year, a classic large-batch system. School of One gives each student a daily "playlist" of tasks with built-in assessments. A teacher who tries a new sequence can see its effect on students at that point right away and roll it out to everyone if it works. (p.190-191)

10. **The large-batch death spiral.** A designer producing thirty drawings before handing them off seems efficient. Then engineering's questions interrupt the designer, and rework follows. Ries finds that product managers and designers in large-batch companies redo their work five or six times per release. Because each handoff brings overhead, people push for even larger batches, and software has no physical limit on batch size. Eventually a "bet the company" release becomes too risky to ship. One company Ries worked at ended up in a crisis and a management change this way. Hospital pharmacies and blood labs show the same pattern, and smaller batches lower their total cost. (p.191-193)

11. **Pull, don't push.** When a dealership uses a bumper to fix your car, the gap in its inventory sends a signal back through Toyota's Parts Distribution Center and regional warehouse to the factory, which makes one more. This is just-in-time production, and it shrinks work-in-progress (WIP) inventory dramatically. Startup WIP is invisible: unfinished designs, unvalidated assumptions, and most business plans are all WIP. (p.193-195)

12. **Hypotheses, not customers, pull the work.** Some people think the Lean Startup means building whatever customers ask for, but customers often do not know what they want. Build-Measure-Learn runs in that order, yet planning runs in reverse: decide what you need to learn, then work backward to the experiment that will teach it. Your hypothesis about the customer pulls work from product development. Everything else is waste. (p.195-196)

13. **Alphabet Energy.** This clean tech startup turns waste heat into electricity with thermoelectric material. By building on silicon wafers, it can use existing semiconductor infrastructure and go from concept to physical product in six weeks. By contrast, SunPower had to build factories and BrightSource raised $291 million for solar plants. Alphabet's first hypothesis was gas turbines at power plants, but utilities are too risk-averse to be early adopters, so it pivoted after three months. After several customer segment pivots, it now targets manufacturers that can test new technology in part of a factory. It had raised about $1 million to date. (p.196-198)

14. **Process is the foundation for culture.** Toyota's real achievement is a learning organization. Lean techniques are only an expression of a high-functioning organization, and process is the base on which culture grows. Without it, efforts to encourage learning and innovation fall flat. The rest of Part Three deals with the human side of this way of working. (p.198)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Batch size | The amount of work that moves from one stage to the next at a time |
| Single-piece flow | A batch size of one; finishing each item fully before starting the next |
| SMED | Single-Minute Exchange of Die, Shigeo Shingo's method for fast machine changeovers |
| Andon cord | A cord on Toyota's line that lets any worker stop production when a problem appears |
| Continuous deployment | Releasing each small change as soon as it is ready, with automated checks and rollback |
| Immune system | IMVU's automated defenses that detect functional and business problems and revert changes |
| Large-batch death spiral | The cycle in which batches keep growing and releases become too risky to ship |
| Pull / Just-in-time | Producing only when the next step signals a need, instead of stockpiling |
| WIP inventory | Work in progress; for a startup, unvalidated assumptions and unfinished designs |
| Hypothesis pull | Letting the hypothesis to be tested decide what gets built |
