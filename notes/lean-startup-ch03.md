## 中文版

### 一句话总结

Startup 的进展不能用按时、按预算、按质量交付来衡量，而要看 validated learning，也就是用真实客户数据证明学到了什么。作者用 IMVU 早期做错 IM add-on 的经历说明，凡是对学习没有贡献的工作都是浪费。

### 核心观点

1. **传统的进度衡量方式不适用。** 作者做工程师和经理时，习惯看工作是否按计划、质量是否达标、成本是否符合预期。但如果做出来的东西没人要，按时按预算也没有意义。另一方面，"我们学到了很多"是执行失败时最老的借口，员工、投资人和组织都没法拿"学习"去兑现。(p.45-46)

2. **Validated learning 的定义。** 作者提出 validated learning 来给"学习"正名。它指用实证方式证明团队发现了关于 startup 当前和未来业务前景的有价值事实。它比市场预测和传统 business plan 更具体、更准确、更快，也是防止 achieving failure（成功执行了一个没有前途的计划）的主要办法。(p.46)

3. **IMVU 当初的"聪明"战略。** 2004 年 IM 市场有几亿用户，但大多不付费。IM 有很强的 network effects，符合 Metcalfe's law（网络价值与用户数的平方成正比）。前三大网络占了 80% 以上的使用量，普遍观点是新的 IM 网络很难进入，因为 switching costs 高，用户换平台得说服朋友一起换。所以 IMVU 决定做一个 add-on，兼容现有 IM 网络，让用户不用换平台、不用学新界面、不用拉朋友过来，并且希望靠每次聊天附带的邀请实现病毒式增长。(p.47-48)

4. **六个月赶出一个糟糕的产品。** 团队给自己定了 180 天的硬期限，作者负责写跨网络兼容的代码。产品复杂，只好大量走捷径，第一版很差。大家害怕损害名声和品牌，但还是按时发布，因为他们更怕另一种结果：做出没人要的东西。作者也提到，很多团队在这时推迟发布，因此得不到需要的反馈。(p.48-49)

5. **发布后什么都没发生。** 根本没人试用，连下载都不愿意。团队每天改进产品，把每天的新用户当作一张成绩单。他们设了很小的收入目标：第一个月 300 美元，之后 350、400 美元，靠求亲友才勉强达到。数字不动，促使他们把客户请到办公室做访谈和 usability test。作者强调，定量目标推动了定性调查，也决定了要问什么问题。(p.49-50)

6. **和客户面对面。** 青少年、重度 IM 用户和技术早期用户愿意尝试，主流用户则觉得太奇怪。一个十几岁的女孩很喜欢定制 avatar，却不懂什么是 IM add-on，更不愿意邀请朋友，怕产品不酷会连累自己。团队做了 single-player 模式，用户玩完还是不肯邀请朋友。后来加了随机匹配陌生人聊天的 ChatNow，用户终于觉得好玩，但又不愿意把陌生人加进自己的 AIM 好友列表。团队以为用户最多用一两个 IM 客户端，一个青少年告诉他们自己同时开着八个。(p.51-53)

7. **战略假设被逐一推翻。** 客户要的是一个独立的 IM 网络，而不是 add-on；学新软件对他们不是障碍；把朋友带到新网络他们反而觉得有意思；他们用 avatar 主要是为了认识新朋友，而不是和老朋友聊天。团队对用户如何使用软件的认知早已过时。(p.53)

8. **扔掉自己的工作。** Pivot 之后，作者几个月里写的几千行兼容代码几乎全部作废。他一直信奉 agile development，本想减少浪费，结果犯了最大的浪费：做出客户不愿意用的产品。他用"至少学到了东西"安慰自己，但真正困扰他的问题是：既然目的是学习，为什么花了这么久？有多少工作真正对学习有贡献？(p.53-54)

9. **Value vs. waste。** 区分创造价值的活动和浪费是 lean manufacturing 的核心问题。Lean thinking 把 value 定义为给客户带来好处。但 startup 连客户是谁、客户看重什么都不知道，所以需要重新定义 value：IMVU 真正的进展是弄清楚什么能为客户创造价值。任何对学习没有贡献的工作都是浪费。(p.54-55)

10. **本可以少做很多。** 那些客户根本发现不了的功能上的争论和排期，如果早点发布就能省掉。作者兼容了十几个 IM 网络，其实支持一个网络就能得到同样的反馈，因为所有网络的用户都同样不喜欢这个产品。更进一步，他们甚至可以什么都不做，只根据功能描述让用户下载，看用户的实际行为。注意这和直接问客户想要什么不同，客户通常事先并不知道自己想要什么。(p.55-56)

11. **学习是 startup 进展的基本单位。** 对学习客户需求不必要的工作都可以砍掉。之所以叫 validated，是因为这种学习总是体现为 startup 核心指标的正向改善，并且有真实客户的实证数据支撑。这也让作者重新思考自己作为产品负责人的工作是什么。(p.56)

12. **Validation 从哪里来。** 光讲失败故事和理论不算证据。IMVU 早期无论怎么改产品，每天购买的新用户比例都接近零。Pivot 之后，指标开始改善，这些数字就是学习真实存在的定量证明。团队把自己的任务定为在愿景和客户愿意接受的东西之间找到结合点，既不屈从于客户说的，也不告诉客户应该要什么。作者说，startup 的生产力应该看获得了多少 validated learning，而不是造了多少东西。(p.56-58)

13. **成功和失败的实验。** 一个成功的例子：把网站上的"avatar chat"整体换成"3D instant messaging"，新用户被自动分成两组，看到新说法的一组注册率更高，也更可能成为长期付费用户。一个失败的例子：花钱请客服给新用户当虚拟导游，结果这些用户并没有更活跃或更愿意付费。经过很多实验，他们终于明白用户想用 IMVU 在网上认识新朋友，而 avatar 能让人在不暴露真实身份的情况下安全地交朋友。之后，凡是让交友更容易的改动都能提高参与度。(p.58)

14. **The audacity of zero。** 讽刺的是，零收入、零用户时融资往往比有一点点数字时更容易，因为零留给人想象空间，小数字却让人怀疑大数字会不会出现。这形成一种糟糕的激励：在确定成功之前一直不拿数据，结果浪费更多、反馈更少。IMVU 月收入在 500 美元左右徘徊几个月后，一些投资人、顾问、员工甚至配偶失去信心，有投资人建议撤回产品、回到 stealth mode。后来曲线虽然像 hockey stick，也只涨到每月几千美元。(p.59-60)

15. **拒绝 success theater。** 用 validated learning 可以减少 audacity of zero 带来的浪费。IMVU 本可以买 Super Bowl 广告或搞大型公关来拉高总数，这叫 vanity metrics 和 success theater，只能制造短暂的 traction 假象，最终业务基本面会说话。(p.60)

16. **IMVU 的教训不是那几个战术。** 学生学 IMVU 案例时，总想记住低质量原型、第一天就收费、用小收入目标问责这些战术。这些有用，但例外很多，不是所有客户都能接受粗糙的原型。Lean Startup 不是战术的集合，而是一套有原则的方法。关键问题从"这个产品能不能做出来"变成"这个产品该不该做"以及"能不能围绕它建立可持续的业务"。要回答这些，就要把 business plan 拆开，逐项用实验检验，也就是用 scientific method。startup 做的每件事都是为了获得 validated learning 的实验。(p.60-61)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| Validated learning | 用真实客户的实证数据证明团队学到了关于业务前景的有价值事实 |
| Achieving failure | 成功执行了一个没有前途的计划 |
| Network effects | 用户越多，产品对每个用户越有价值 |
| Metcalfe's law | 网络价值与参与者数量的平方成正比 |
| Switching costs | 用户从一个产品换到另一个产品要付出的代价 |
| Value vs. waste | Lean 的核心区分；在 startup 里，对学习没有贡献的工作就是浪费 |
| Audacity of zero | 零数据比小数据更容易吸引投资的现象，会诱使团队推迟收集数据 |
| Success theater | 为了显得成功而做的表面功夫 |
| Vanity metrics | 看起来好看但不能反映真实进展的指标（后面章节展开） |

### 我的思考题

- 你做过的项目里，有哪些工作事后看对"学到客户真正需要什么"没有贡献？
- IMVU 本可以只描述功能、不写代码就测试需求。你的课程项目能不能用类似方法先验证假设？
- Audacity of zero 在学生创业比赛或路演中存在吗？评委更容易被零数据的大故事打动吗？

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

A startup's progress should be measured by validated learning, meaning evidence from real customers about what the team has learned, rather than by on-time, on-budget, high-quality delivery. Ries uses IMVU's failed IM add-on to show that any work that does not contribute to learning is waste.

### Key Points

1. **Traditional progress measures don't fit.** As an engineer and manager, Ries judged progress by whether work followed the plan, met quality standards, and stayed on budget. But if nobody wants the product, none of that matters. At the same time, "we learned a lot" is the oldest excuse for failed execution, and employees, investors, and organizations cannot cash in on learning. (p.45-46)

2. **Defining validated learning.** Ries uses the term to rehabilitate learning. Validated learning means demonstrating empirically that a team has discovered valuable truths about a startup's present and future business prospects. It is more concrete, accurate, and fast than market forecasts or classic business plans, and it is the main defense against achieving failure, which is successfully executing a plan that leads nowhere. (p.46)

3. **IMVU's "brilliant" strategy.** In 2004 the IM market had hundreds of millions of users, most of whom paid nothing. IM has strong network effects and is thought to follow Metcalfe's law, where a network's value grows with the square of its users. The top three networks held more than 80 percent of usage, and conventional wisdom said a new network was nearly impossible to launch because switching costs were high: users would have to bring their friends along. So IMVU built an add-on that worked with existing IM networks, so users would not have to switch, learn a new interface, or move friends, and it would spread virally through invitations in every chat. (p.47-48)

4. **Six months to a bad product.** The team set a hard 180-day deadline, and Ries wrote the code for cross-network compatibility. The product was so complex they cut many corners, and the first version was terrible. They feared damage to their reputations and brand but launched anyway, because they feared building something nobody wanted even more. Ries notes that many teams delay launch at this point and lose the feedback they need. (p.48-49)

5. **At launch, nothing happened.** Nobody tried the product. People would not even download it. The team improved the product daily and treated each day's new customers as a report card. They set small revenue targets: $300 in the first month, then $350 and $400, barely met by asking friends and family. When the numbers would not move, they brought customers in for interviews and usability tests. Ries points out that the quantitative targets drove the qualitative research and shaped which questions they asked. (p.49-50)

6. **Face to face with customers.** Teens, heavy IM users, and tech early adopters would engage, while mainstream users found IMVU too weird. One teenage girl enjoyed customizing her avatar but had no idea what an IM add-on was and refused to invite a friend in case the product turned out uncool. The team built a single-player mode, and users still would not invite friends. Then they added ChatNow, which randomly matched strangers, and users finally had fun, but they did not want strangers on their AIM buddy lists. The team assumed people ran one or two IM clients. One teenager said she ran eight. (p.51-53)

7. **The strategy fell apart piece by piece.** Customers wanted a standalone IM network, not an add-on. Learning new software was no barrier. Bringing friends to a new network was something they enjoyed. And they wanted avatars mainly to meet new people, not to chat with existing friends. The team's model of how people used software was years out of date. (p.53)

8. **Throwing work away.** After the pivot, nearly all of the thousands of lines of compatibility code Ries had written were discarded. He had followed agile development to reduce waste and still committed the biggest waste of all: building a product customers refused to use. He comforted himself that at least they had learned something, but the harder question remained. If the goal was learning, why did it take so long, and how much of the effort actually contributed to it? (p.53-54)

9. **Value vs. waste.** Separating value-creating work from waste is the central question of lean manufacturing. Lean thinking defines value as benefit to the customer. In a startup, though, the customer and what they value are unknown, so Ries needed a new definition. IMVU's real progress was learning what created value for customers. Anything that did not contribute to that learning was waste. (p.54-55)

10. **They could have done far less.** Debates over features customers never discovered could have been avoided by shipping sooner. Ries built compatibility for more than a dozen IM networks, when one would have produced the same feedback, since users on every network disliked the product equally. They might even have tested demand without building anything, by offering downloads based on proposed features and watching behavior. This differs from asking customers what they want, which they usually do not know in advance. (p.55-56)

11. **Learning is the unit of progress.** Work that is not needed to learn what customers want can be cut. Ries calls it validated learning because it always shows up as improvement in the startup's core metrics and is backed by empirical data from real customers. This forced him to rethink his job as head of product development. (p.56)

12. **Where validation comes from.** Stories and theories about failure are not evidence. In IMVU's early days, the share of new customers who bought stayed near zero no matter how the product improved. After the pivot, the metrics began to move, and that movement was the quantitative proof that the learning was real. The team saw its job as finding a synthesis between its vision and what customers would accept, without caving to what customers said they wanted or telling them what they ought to want. Startup productivity should be judged by validated learning gained, not by how much gets built. (p.56-58)

13. **Experiments that worked and ones that didn't.** In one split test, IMVU replaced "avatar chat" with "3D instant messaging" across the site. The group that saw the new wording signed up more and was more likely to become long-term paying customers. In a failed experiment, they paid customer service agents to act as virtual tour guides, and those customers were no more likely to become active or paying. After many experiments, they realized customers wanted to use IMVU to make new friends online, and avatars let people do that safely without revealing their real identity. From then on, changes that made it easier to find and keep friends raised engagement. (p.58)

14. **The audacity of zero.** Oddly, it is often easier to raise money with zero revenue and zero customers than with small numbers, since zero leaves room for imagination while small numbers raise doubts. This creates an incentive to avoid collecting data until success seems certain, which increases waste and cuts off feedback. After IMVU's revenue hovered around $500 a month for several months, some investors, advisers, employees, and even spouses lost faith, and some investors suggested pulling the product and returning to stealth mode. Even when growth began to look like a hockey stick, it only reached a few thousand dollars a month. (p.59-60)

15. **Avoiding success theater.** Validated learning reduces the waste caused by the audacity of zero. IMVU could have bought a Super Bowl ad or run flashy PR to inflate its gross numbers. That kind of vanity metric and success theater only creates a short-lived illusion of traction before the fundamentals catch up. (p.60)

16. **The lesson is not the tactics.** Students studying the IMVU case tend to focus on tactics: low-quality early prototypes, charging from day one, and small revenue targets for accountability. These help, but there are many exceptions, and not every customer will accept a rough prototype. The Lean Startup is a principled approach, not a set of tactics. The key question shifts from whether a product can be built to whether it should be built and whether a sustainable business can grow around it. Answering that requires breaking a business plan into parts and testing each one empirically, which is the scientific method. Everything a startup does is an experiment aimed at validated learning. (p.60-61)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Validated learning | Empirical proof, from real customer data, that a team has learned something valuable about its business prospects |
| Achieving failure | Successfully executing a plan that leads nowhere |
| Network effects | A product becomes more valuable to each user as more people use it |
| Metcalfe's law | A network's value is proportional to the square of its participants |
| Switching costs | What users give up to move from one product to another |
| Value vs. waste | The core lean distinction. In a startup, work that does not produce learning is waste |
| Audacity of zero | Zero data attracts investors more easily than small data, tempting teams to delay collecting it |
| Success theater | Work done to look successful rather than make progress |
| Vanity metrics | Numbers that look good but do not reflect real progress (covered later) |

### Discussion Questions

- In projects you have done, which work turned out to contribute nothing to learning what users actually needed?
- IMVU could have tested demand by describing features before writing code. Could your course project validate its assumptions the same way?
- Does the audacity of zero show up in student pitch competitions? Are judges more impressed by a big story with no data?
