> Introduction ｜ PDF p.11-21

## 中文版

### 一句话总结

创业成功靠的不是天赋或运气，而是一套可以学习、可以教授的流程。作者从自己的失败和 IMVU 的经历出发，提出 Lean Startup，并给出贯穿全书的五条原则。

### 核心观点

1. **作者的第一次创业失败。** 作者早年和朋友做了一个让大学生建立在线个人档案、再分享给雇主的产品。dot-com 泡沫破裂后钱花光了，融不到资，两个 cofounder 在雨中争吵后各走各路。作者回头看，当时产品、团队、技术和时机似乎都对，失败的原因是不知道该用什么流程把产品洞察变成一家好公司。(p.11-12)

2. **"坚持加天才就能成功"是个神话。** 媒体反复讲的创业故事是 selection bias 和事后合理化的产物。现实是大多数 startup 会失败，大多数新产品不成功。这个神话的吸引力在于它让失败有了现成的借口。作者的结论相反：真正重要的是那些无聊的细节，成功可以通过正确的流程被"设计"出来，所以能学，也能教。(p.12-13)

3. **Entrepreneurship 是一种管理。** 人们通常觉得创业很酷，管理很无聊，作者要求读者放下这种成见。(p.13)

4. **IMVU 的做法看起来"全错"。** 2004 年，一群刚经历公开失败的创始人跟随 Will Harvey 做 IMVU，用 avatar 改变人们的社交方式，虚拟服装和家具由用户自己制作并互相出售。作者是 cofounder 兼 CTO。他们先做了一个满是 bug、甚至会让电脑崩溃的 minimum viable product，提前发布，还收费。之后每天发布几十个新版本。他们常和早期用户交流，但不照着用户说的做，而是把用户反馈当作信息来源之一，更多是在用户身上跑实验。(p.13-14)

5. **Lean Startup 的来源。** 作者是程序员出身，职业生涯中反复遇到辛苦做出的产品在市场上失败。他起初以为是技术问题，技术上的修补却带来更多失败。在 IMVU，投资人兼顾问 Steve Blank 提出 Customer Development，主张 startup 的商业和营销职能应该和工程一样有严谨的方法。作者又研究了源自 Toyota Production System 的 lean manufacturing，稍作调整后用来解释自己的经验。Lean Startup 就是把 lean thinking 用在创新过程上，同时也借鉴了 design thinking 和 agile development。(p.14-15)

6. **IMVU 的结果和运动的扩散。** IMVU 用户创建了超过 6000 万个 avatar，2011 年年收入超过 5000 万美元，员工一百多人，虚拟商品目录超过 600 万件，每天新增 7000 多件，几乎都是用户做的。作者向其他人介绍这些经验时常被质疑行不通，于是开始写博客 Startup Lessons Learned 并四处演讲。后来 Lean Startup 在一百多个城市有了实践社群，作者还给美国陆军的 CIO 讲过。(p.15-17)

7. **Lean Startup 的五条原则**（贯穿全书三个部分）：
   - **Entrepreneurs are everywhere**：只要是在极度不确定的条件下创造新产品或服务的组织，就是 startup，不论公司大小和行业。
   - **Entrepreneurship is management**：startup 是一个组织，需要专门适应不确定性的管理方法。作者认为"entrepreneur"应该成为依赖创新的现代公司里的一个正式职位。
   - **Validated learning**：startup 存在的目的是学会如何建立可持续的业务，这种学习可以通过频繁实验来科学地验证。
   - **Build-Measure-Learn**：把想法做成产品，衡量客户反应，再决定 pivot 还是 persevere。所有流程都应该服务于加快这个循环。
   - **Innovation accounting**：关注怎么衡量进展、怎么设 milestones、怎么排优先级，这需要一种为 startup 设计的新型会计方法。(p.17-18)

8. **Startup 失败的两个原因。** 第一，被好计划、扎实战略和详尽市场调研吸引。这些在稳定环境里有用，但 startup 连客户是谁、产品该是什么都不知道，预测只有在长期稳定的经营历史下才准确。第二，看到传统管理失效后，有人走向另一个极端，信奉"Just Do It"，以为混乱就是答案，这同样行不通。(p.18-19)

9. **全书结构：Vision, Steer, Accelerate。**
   - **Vision**：论证为什么需要 entrepreneurial management，定义谁是创业者、什么是 startup，并提出 validated learning。
   - **Steer**：详细走一遍 Build-Measure-Learn 循环，从 leap-of-faith assumptions 开始，讲 MVP、新的会计体系，以及如何决定 pivot 或 persevere。
   - **Accelerate**：讲如何在规模扩大时仍然快速跑完循环，包括 small batches、组织设计、产品增长方式，以及在大公司里应用这些原则。(p.20)

10. **管理的第二个世纪。** 社会已经知道怎么管理大公司、怎么造实物产品，但面对创业和创新仍在摸黑前进，要么依赖少数"伟人"，要么分析过度。作者希望把创业和创新放到严谨的基础上。(p.20-21)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| Minimum viable product (MVP) | 尽早发布的早期产品，哪怕粗糙，用来尽快获得真实反馈 |
| Customer Development | Steve Blank 提出的方法，要求 startup 的商业和营销职能也有严谨流程 |
| Lean thinking | 源自 Toyota 的思想，Lean Startup 把它用在创新过程上 |
| Validated learning | 通过实验验证的、关于如何建立可持续业务的学习 |
| Build-Measure-Learn | startup 的核心循环：做出产品，衡量反应，学习后决定下一步 |
| Pivot / Persevere | 调整方向，或坚持当前战略 |
| Innovation accounting | 为 startup 设计的衡量进展、设定 milestones 和排优先级的方法 |
| Leap-of-faith assumptions | 需要被严格检验的关键假设（Part Two 展开） |

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Startup success comes from a process that can be learned and taught, not from talent or luck. Drawing on his own failure and his time at IMVU, Ries introduces the Lean Startup and the five principles behind the rest of the book.

### Key Points

1. **Ries's first startup failure.** Early in his career, Ries co-built a product that let college students create online profiles to share with employers. After the dot-com bubble burst, the money ran out and fundraising failed, and he and his cofounder split after an argument in the rain. Looking back, the product, team, technology, and timing all seemed right. What they lacked was a process for turning product insight into a great company. (p.11-12)

2. **The "grit plus genius" story is a myth.** The popular startup narrative is shaped by selection bias and after-the-fact rationalization. In reality most startups fail and most new products do not succeed. The myth is appealing because it gives failure a ready excuse. Ries argues the opposite: the boring details matter most, and success can be engineered through the right process, which means it can be learned and taught. (p.12-13)

3. **Entrepreneurship is a kind of management.** People tend to see entrepreneurship as exciting and management as dull. Ries asks readers to drop that assumption. (p.13)

4. **IMVU did everything "wrong."** In 2004, a group of founders fresh off a public failure followed Will Harvey to build IMVU, an avatar-based way for people to connect, where customers would make the virtual clothing and furniture and sell it to each other. Ries was cofounder and CTO. They shipped a buggy, crash-prone minimum viable product early, charged money for it, and then released new versions dozens of times a day. They talked with early adopters often but did not simply do what those users asked. Customer input was one source of information, and the team ran experiments on customers far more than it catered to them. (p.13-14)

5. **Where the Lean Startup came from.** Ries grew up programming and kept seeing products he worked hard on fail in the market. He first treated these as technical problems, and technical fixes led to more failure. At IMVU, investor and adviser Steve Blank was promoting Customer Development, the idea that a startup's business and marketing functions deserve a method as rigorous as engineering. Ries also studied lean manufacturing from the Toyota Production System and adapted it to explain what he saw. The Lean Startup applies lean thinking to innovation and also draws on design thinking and agile development. (p.14-15)

6. **IMVU's results and the spread of the movement.** IMVU customers created more than 60 million avatars. The company earned over $50 million in revenue in 2011, had more than a hundred employees, and had a virtual goods catalog of over 6 million items, with more than 7,000 added daily, nearly all made by customers. When Ries shared his approach, people often doubted it could work, so he started the blog Startup Lessons Learned and spoke widely. Lean Startup communities formed in more than a hundred cities, and he even briefed the U.S. Army's chief information officer. (p.15-17)

7. **The five principles of the Lean Startup** (used across all three parts):
   - **Entrepreneurs are everywhere**: any organization creating new products or services under extreme uncertainty is a startup, whatever its size or industry.
   - **Entrepreneurship is management**: a startup is an institution and needs management suited to uncertainty. Ries thinks "entrepreneur" should be a job title in modern companies that depend on innovation.
   - **Validated learning**: startups exist to learn how to build a sustainable business, and that learning can be validated through frequent experiments.
   - **Build-Measure-Learn**: turn ideas into products, measure customer response, then decide whether to pivot or persevere. Every process should speed up this loop.
   - **Innovation accounting**: measuring progress, setting milestones, and prioritizing work require a new kind of accounting built for startups. (p.17-18)

8. **Two reasons startups fail.** First, the pull of a good plan, solid strategy, and thorough market research. These worked in stable settings, but startups do not yet know their customer or their product, and forecasts only work with a long, stable operating history. Second, some people react to that by adopting a "Just Do It" attitude and treating chaos as the answer. That fails too. (p.18-19)

9. **How the book is organized: Vision, Steer, Accelerate.**
   - **Vision**: the case for entrepreneurial management, who counts as an entrepreneur, what a startup is, and validated learning.
   - **Steer**: one full pass through Build-Measure-Learn, starting with leap-of-faith assumptions, then MVPs, a new accounting system, and the pivot-or-persevere decision.
   - **Accelerate**: how to move through the loop quickly even while scaling, including small batches, organizational design, how products grow, and applying the ideas inside large companies. (p.20)

10. **Management's second century.** Society knows how to run big companies and build physical products, but with startups and innovation it is still guessing, either relying on a few "great men" or over-analyzing. Ries wants to put entrepreneurship and innovation on a rigorous footing. (p.20-21)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Minimum viable product (MVP) | An early, often rough product released to get real feedback quickly |
| Customer Development | Steve Blank's method giving a startup's business and marketing functions a rigorous process |
| Lean thinking | Toyota-derived ideas that the Lean Startup applies to innovation |
| Validated learning | Learning about how to build a sustainable business, confirmed through experiments |
| Build-Measure-Learn | The core startup loop: build, measure the response, learn, and decide what to do next |
| Pivot / Persevere | Change course, or stay with the current strategy |
| Innovation accounting | A startup-specific way to measure progress, set milestones, and prioritize |
| Leap-of-faith assumptions | Key assumptions that need rigorous testing (covered in Part Two) |

### Discussion Questions

- How many startup success stories you know might reflect selection bias? What happened to similar companies that failed?
- IMVU talked to customers but did not follow their requests. Does that conflict with being customer-centered?
- Which of the five principles differs most from how you used to think about startups?
