## 中文版

### Part Two 导读

- **Startup 是把想法变成产品的催化剂。** 客户使用产品后会产生反馈和数据，既有定性的（喜欢什么、不喜欢什么），也有定量的（多少人在用、觉得有价值）。对 startup 来说，这些信息比钱、奖项或媒体报道更重要，因为它们会改变下一轮的想法。(p.80)
- **Build-Measure-Learn 是整个方法的核心。** 工程师擅长高效地做东西，有些经理擅长在白板上做战略，很多创业者执着于最好的点子或数据本身。作者认为单独哪一环都不是最重要的，关键是缩短走完整个循环的总时间，这就是 Part Two 说的"掌舵"。(p.81)
- **三个环节各有重点。** 先找出 leap-of-faith assumptions，其中最重要的是 value hypothesis 和 growth hypothesis；然后用 MVP 尽快进入 Build 阶段，MVP 要能测量影响，所以必须拿到潜在客户面前；Measure 阶段用 innovation accounting 和 learning milestones 判断是否真的有进展，并区分 vanity metrics 和 actionable metrics（第 7 章）；最后面对最难的问题：pivot 还是 persevere。(p.82-83)
- **计划的顺序是反过来的。** 执行顺序是 Build-Measure-Learn，但规划时要先想清楚需要学到什么，再决定要衡量什么，最后才决定要做什么产品。Part Two 的所有方法都是为了缩短这个循环的时间。(p.83)

### 一句话总结

每个商业计划都建立在假设之上，其中少数几个 leap-of-faith assumptions 决定了企业的成败。创业者要把它们清楚地写出来，走出办公室去接触真实客户来检验，同时避免"埋头就做"和"分析瘫痪"两个极端。

### 核心观点

1. **Facebook 为什么能用很小的用户量融到大钱。** 2004 年 Facebook 只有 15 万注册用户，收入很少，却先融到 50 万美元，不到一年后又融了 1,270 万美元。打动投资人的是两件事：超过一半的用户每天都回来，这验证了 value hypothesis；2004 年 2 月 4 日上线，当月月底哈佛近四分之三的本科生都在用，而且没花一分钱营销，这验证了 growth hypothesis。(p.84-85)

2. **Facebook 和 dot-com 泡沫公司不一样。** 当时很多人批评 Facebook 没有 business model，像 dot-com 时代花钱买"眼球"的公司。作者指出那些公司本质是中间商，花钱买用户注意力再转卖。Facebook 用的是另一种 engine of growth：获客不花钱，高参与度让它每天积累大量注意力，唯一的问题只是广告主愿意付多少。(p.85)

3. **成功故事的经验不能照搬。** 从 Facebook 身上学到的，是早期不要收费，还是不要做营销？这些问题没法抽象地回答，每种做法都能找到大量反例。startup 需要做实验来判断什么方法适合自己的处境。战略的作用是帮助找出该问的正确问题。(p.85)

4. **战略建立在假设之上。** 商业计划把一组假设当作已知前提，再推导如何实现愿景。这些假设既未被证明，也常常是错的，所以 startup 早期的目标就是尽快检验它们。创业者面临两个挑战：建立一个能系统检验假设的组织，并且在严格检验的同时不丢掉整体愿景。(p.85-86)

5. **少数假设才是真正的 leap of faith。** 计划里的大多数假设很普通，来自行业经验或简单推理，比如广告主愿意为用户注意力付钱。藏在其中的少数几个假设才需要勇气说出口，比如"客户非常想用我们这样的产品"或"超市会上架我们的产品"。整个企业的成败都押在它们上面，所以叫 leap of faith。(p.86)

6. **类比论证会掩盖真正的 leap of faith。** 很多计划的逻辑是：以前的技术 X 靠属性 Z 赢得了市场 Y，我们的新技术 X2 也有属性 Z，所以能赢得市场 Y2。这种类比的目的是让生意看起来风险更低，方便说服投资人和员工。作者把一个"progressive rendering"的例子改写得更直白后，隐含的假设就暴露了：真的有一大批客户现在就想要这个产品、买得起、只是因为速度太慢用不上吗？改写后的结论是应该先做实证检验，而不是先把技术做出来。(p.86-87)

7. **Analogs 和 antilogs。** 风投 Randy Komisar 用 iPod 解释这个框架：Sony Walkman 是 analog，已经证明人们愿意戴耳机在公共场合听音乐；Napster 是 antilog，说明人们愿意下载音乐却不愿付钱。从 analogs 和 antilogs 中剩下的、还没有答案的问题，才是真正的 leap of faith。对 iPod 来说，其中之一就是人们会为音乐付费。(p.88)

8. **"天时地利"解释不了成功。** Henry Ford 那个年代有将近五百家汽车创业公司，身处历史上最大的市场机会之一，绝大多数却没赚到钱。Facebook 的早期竞争对手也有先发优势，但没有用。成功者的区别在于有眼光、能力和工具去发现计划中哪些部分有效、哪些是错的，并据此调整战略。(p.88-89)

9. **Value 和 growth 都要看本质。** 作者用"价值"而非"利润"来说，因为创业者也包括非营利组织、公共部门和大公司内部的变革推动者。有些组织短期很赚钱却在毁灭价值，比如庞氏骗局、Enron 和 Lehman Brothers。增长也一样，靠不断融资和大量付费广告、却没有创造价值的产品的增长，是作者说的 success theater，用增长的表象假装成功。innovation accounting（第 7 章）的目标之一就是区分这种假 startup 和真正的创新者。传统会计的标准不能预测 startup 的前景，Amazon 就曾在突破前长期巨额亏损。(p.89-90)

10. **早期战略靠直觉，要走出去变成数据。** innovation accounting 也需要一个量化的财务模型，但 startup 最早期没有足够数据，战略多半凭直觉，这没问题。要把直觉变成数据，创业者需要按 Steve Blank 的说法 get out of the building，开始学习。(p.90)

11. **Genchi gembutsu：亲自去现场看。** 这是 Toyota Production System 的核心原则，意思是基于第一手了解做决策。2004 款 Sienna 的 chief engineer Yuji Yokoya 对北美市场不熟，于是开车跑遍美国 50 个州、加拿大所有省和地区以及墨西哥各地，总里程超过 53,000 英里，边开车边观察和访谈客户。他发现小孩才是 minivan 真正的"主人"，于是在车内舒适性上投入了不寻常的预算。2004 款销量比 2003 款高 60%。不过作者提醒，这是成熟企业擅长的 sustaining innovation；startup 的不确定性高得多，早期接触客户更多是为了找出哪些假设最需要马上检验。(p.90-92)

12. **Get out of the building。** 作者提醒数字背后都是人，即使是 B2B，企业也是由一个个人组成的。第一步是确认客户真的有一个值得解决的重大问题。Intuit 创始人 Scott Cook 在 1982 年没有先做市场研究，而是拿起 Palo Alto 和伊利诺伊州 Winnetka 的电话簿随机打电话，问人们怎么管理财务，验证"手工付账单很烦"这个 leap-of-faith 问题。这些对话没有问产品功能，因为当时普通人对个人电脑了解太少，但确认了背后有一个很大的主流市场。(p.92-93)

13. **Customer archetype 是假设，不是事实。** 早期接触客户的目的不是得到确定答案，而是粗略地确认我们理解潜在客户和他们的问题，然后写一份 customer archetype，把目标客户具体化，用来指导团队日常的优先级决策。传统设计方法本身很注重实验和迭代，但设计公司最后交付一个完整成品后学习就停止了，这不适合 startup。Lean UX 认为 archetype 在被 validated learning 证明之前都只是临时的。(p.93-94)

14. **两个极端：埋头就做和 analysis paralysis。** "just do it" 派只跟客户聊几句就开干，因为客户自己也说不清想要什么，他们很容易自我欺骗。另一派陷入 analysis paralysis，不停地完善计划。多数计划的问题在于所依据的事实是错的，而这些错误往往取决于产品和客户之间微妙的互动，在白板上发现不了。什么时候该停止分析、开始动手？答案是 MVP，也就是第 6 章的主题。(p.94)

### 关键术语

| 术语 | 解释 |
|---|---|
| Leap-of-faith assumptions | 商业计划中风险最大、整个企业成败所系的假设 |
| Value hypothesis | 客户是否觉得产品有价值 |
| Growth hypothesis | 产品如何获得新客户、实现增长 |

| Innovation accounting | 专为 startup 设计的衡量进展的方法（第 7 章） |

## English Version

### Part Two Overview

- **A startup is a catalyst that turns ideas into products.** As customers use those products, they generate feedback and data, both qualitative (what they like and dislike) and quantitative (how many use it and find it valuable). For a startup this information matters more than money, awards, or press, because it reshapes the next round of ideas. (p.80)
- **Build-Measure-Learn is the core of the method.** Engineers are trained to build efficiently, some managers excel at whiteboard strategy, and many entrepreneurs fixate on the best idea or on data. Ries argues that no single step matters most. What matters is minimizing the total time through the whole loop, which is what Part Two means by steering. (p.81)
- **Each step has its own focus.** First identify the leap-of-faith assumptions, above all the value hypothesis and growth hypothesis. Then enter Build quickly with an MVP, which must be measurable and therefore put in front of real potential customers. In Measure, use innovation accounting and learning milestones to judge real progress, and separate vanity metrics from actionable metrics (Chapter 7). Finally, face the hardest question: pivot or persevere. (p.82-83)
- **Planning runs in reverse.** The activities happen in Build-Measure-Learn order, but planning starts with what we need to learn, then what we need to measure, and only then what product to build. Every technique in Part Two aims to shorten the loop. (p.83)

### One-Sentence Summary

Every business plan rests on assumptions, and a few leap-of-faith assumptions decide whether the venture succeeds. Entrepreneurs should state them plainly, get out of the building to test them with real customers, and avoid both rushing to build and analysis paralysis.

### Key Points

1. **Why Facebook raised big money with modest usage.** In 2004 Facebook had 150,000 registered users and little revenue, yet it raised $500,000 and then $12.7 million less than a year later. Investors were impressed by two facts. More than half of users came back every day, which validated the value hypothesis. It launched on February 4, 2004, and by the end of that month almost three-quarters of Harvard undergraduates were using it with no marketing spend, which validated the growth hypothesis. (p.84-85)

2. **Facebook was not a dot-com rerun.** Critics said Facebook had no business model and looked like the dot-com companies that bought "eyeballs." Ries says those companies were really middlemen that paid for attention and planned to resell it. Facebook ran on a different engine of growth. It paid nothing to acquire users, and high engagement meant it gathered huge amounts of attention every day. The only open question was how much advertisers would pay. (p.85)

3. **Success stories do not transfer directly.** Is the lesson of Facebook that startups should not charge early, or that they should never spend on marketing? Such questions cannot be answered in the abstract, since every technique has countless counterexamples. Startups need experiments to find out what works in their own situation. The job of strategy is to help identify the right questions. (p.85)

4. **Strategy is built on assumptions.** A business plan takes a set of assumptions as given and shows how to reach the vision. Those assumptions are unproven and often wrong, so a startup's early work should test them as fast as possible. The entrepreneur has two challenges: build an organization that can test assumptions systematically, and do it without losing sight of the overall vision. (p.85-86)

5. **Only a few assumptions are true leaps of faith.** Most assumptions in a plan are ordinary facts from industry experience or simple deductions, such as advertisers paying for attention. Hidden among them are a few that take courage to state, like "customers badly want a product like ours" or "supermarkets will carry our product." The whole venture rests on these, which is why they are called leaps of faith. (p.86)

6. **Arguments by analogy hide the real leap of faith.** Many plans argue that technology X won market Y because of attribute Z, so new technology X2 with the same attribute will win market Y2. Such analogies exist to make a business look less risky and to win over investors and employees. When Ries rewrites a "progressive rendering" pitch in blunt terms, the hidden assumption appears: are there really many customers who want this now, can afford it, and are held back only by slow rendering? The rewrite shows that empirical testing should come before building the technology. (p.86-87)

7. **Analogs and antilogs.** Venture capitalist Randy Komisar explains this framework with the iPod. The Sony Walkman was an analog, since it had already shown people would listen to music in public with earphones. Napster was an antilog, showing people would download music but not pay for it. The unanswered questions left after studying analogs and antilogs are the real leaps of faith. For the iPod, one of them was that people would pay for music. (p.88)

8. **"Right place, right time" does not explain success.** Henry Ford was joined by nearly five hundred other auto entrepreneurs, all sitting on one of history's biggest market opportunities, and most made no money. Facebook's early rivals also had a head start that turned out not to matter. The winners had the foresight, ability, and tools to find which parts of their plans worked and which did not, and to adapt. (p.88-89)

9. **Look at the substance of value and growth.** Ries speaks of value rather than profit because entrepreneurs include nonprofit founders, public sector innovators, and internal change agents. Some organizations are very profitable in the short term but destroy value, such as Ponzi schemes, Enron, and Lehman Brothers. Growth can also be hollow. A business that grows through constant fund-raising and paid ads without a value-creating product is engaged in success theater, using the look of growth to seem successful. Innovation accounting (Chapter 7) aims to separate these false startups from real innovators. Traditional accounting standards do not predict a startup's prospects well, as Amazon's large losses on its way to success show. (p.89-90)

10. **Early strategy runs on hunches, so go collect data.** Innovation accounting still needs a quantitative financial model, but in the earliest days there is too little data, so plans are guided by intuition, and that is fine. To turn instinct into data, entrepreneurs have to get out of the building, in Steve Blank's phrase, and start learning. (p.90)

11. **Genchi gembutsu: go and see for yourself.** This core principle of the Toyota Production System means basing decisions on firsthand knowledge. Yuji Yokoya, chief engineer for the 2004 Sienna minivan, had little North American experience, so he drove through all 50 U.S. states, all of Canada's provinces and territories, and all parts of Mexico, more than 53,000 miles in total, observing and talking with customers. He learned that kids really rule the minivan, so he spent an unusual share of the budget on interior comfort. 2004 sales were 60 percent higher than 2003. Ries notes this was a sustaining innovation, which established companies do well. Startups face far more uncertainty, and their early customer contact mainly shows which assumptions need testing most urgently. (p.90-92)

12. **Get out of the building.** Ries reminds entrepreneurs that metrics are people too, and even in B2B, organizations are made of individuals. The first step is to confirm that the customer has a significant problem worth solving. When Scott Cook started Intuit in 1982, he skipped market research and called people at random from the Palo Alto and Winnetka, Illinois, phone books to ask how they managed their finances. He was testing one leap-of-faith question: do people find paying bills by hand frustrating? He did not ask about product features, since most people then knew too little about PCs to have an opinion, but he confirmed a large mainstream market existed. (p.92-93)

13. **A customer archetype is a hypothesis, not a fact.** Early customer contact is meant to confirm, at a rough level, that we understand potential customers and their problems. That understanding feeds a customer archetype, a short document that humanizes the target customer and guides the team's daily priorities. Traditional design work is highly iterative, but design agencies end with one big deliverable and then stop learning, which does not work for startups. Lean UX treats the archetype as provisional until validated learning shows the company can serve that customer sustainably. (p.93-94)

14. **Two extremes: just do it and analysis paralysis.** "Just do it" founders start building after a few quick conversations, and because customers do not really know what they want, they can easily fool themselves. Others fall into analysis paralysis and keep refining the plan. Most plans fail because their underlying facts are wrong, and those errors depend on subtle interactions between product and customer that cannot be seen at a whiteboard. The answer to when to stop analyzing and start building is the MVP, the subject of Chapter 6. (p.94)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Leap-of-faith assumptions | The riskiest assumptions in a plan, on which the whole venture depends |
| Value hypothesis | Whether customers find the product valuable |
| Growth hypothesis | How the product will attract new customers and grow |
| Analogs / Antilogs | Randy Komisar's framework: precedents that confirm an assumption, and precedents that point the other way |
| Success theater | Using the appearance of growth (fund-raising, paid ads) to look successful without creating value |
| Innovation accounting | A startup-specific way to measure progress (Chapter 7) |
| Genchi gembutsu | Toyota's "go and see for yourself" principle of deciding from firsthand knowledge |
| Get out of the building | Steve Blank's point that facts about customers and markets exist only outside the office |
| Customer archetype | A short document that humanizes the target customer, treated as a hypothesis to validate |
| Lean UX | An approach that treats user experience design as ongoing experimentation and iteration |
| Analysis paralysis | Endlessly analyzing and refining a plan instead of acting |

### Discussion Questions

- Take a startup pitch you know and rewrite its analogy as a blunt leap-of-faith statement. What assumption comes to the surface?
- Yokoya's genchi gembutsu served a sustaining innovation. How does the role of firsthand observation change for a startup facing much higher uncertainty?
- Do you lean more toward "just do it" or toward analysis paralysis? What signal could tell you it is time to switch?
