# The Lean Startup, Chapter 8: Pivot (or Persevere)

> Part Two: STEER ｜ PDF p.147-174

## 中文版

### 一句话总结

每个创业者最终都要面对一个问题：当前的战略假设是否足够有进展，还是需要一次 pivot。Pivot 是一种有结构的方向修正，用来检验关于产品、战略和 engine of growth 的新的基本假设。Innovation accounting 能让 pivot 来得更快，而 pivot 需要勇气和固定的决策机制。

### 核心观点

1. **Pivot 的定义。** Pivot 是一种有结构的 course correction，目的是检验一个新的基本假设，涉及产品、战略和 engine of growth。作者强调 Lean Startup 不是一个机械的决策公式，vision、直觉和判断无法也不应该被去掉。科学方法的作用是通过反复检验来改善人的判断。最伤害创造力的，是错误地坚持下去。(p.147-148)

2. **Votizen 的多次 pivot。** David Binetti 想提高公民的政治参与。第一个 MVP 是一个经过验证的选民社交网络，花了约 1,200 美元和三个月。他列出了四个 leap-of-faith：registration、activation、retention、referral。
   - 初始结果：注册 5%，验证选民身份 17%，数据太少看不出留存和推荐。(p.148-149)
   - 又花两个月和 5,000 美元做 split test 和优化，注册升到 17%，激活超过 90%，但留存只有 5%，推荐 4%。(p.149-150)
   - 再优化三个月，留存 8%，推荐 6%。前后八个月花了 2 万美元，增长模型仍不成立。(p.150)

3. **David 避开 land of the living dead 的两个原因。** 第一，他尽早发布并迭代，所以在公司成立八个月时就面对了 pivot 决策，投入越多越难转向。第二，他一开始就明确写下了 leap-of-faith 问题并做了量化预测，所以不能事后宣布成功，虽然激活率和总使用量看起来都不错。他也没做过早的 PR，所以承认失败不会公开丢脸。学习的前提是失败，先发布再看结果的做法只能保证你看到会怎样。(p.151-152)

4. **Zoom-in、customer segment、platform 三次 pivot。**
   - **Zoom-in pivot**：客户反馈是喜欢这个理念、看重选民验证，但社交网络里没人，不想回来。David 把产品变成 @2gov，一个 social lobbying platform，让用户通过 Twitter 等现有社交网络联系议员，再把数字消息转成纸质信件送到国会。新的增长模型从 sticky 变成付费。结果注册 42%，激活 83%，留存 21%，推荐 54%，但愿意付费的活动人士不到 1%。累计花了 5 万美元、12 个月。(p.152-153)
   - **Customer segment pivot**：功能不变，付费方从个人换成大型组织、筹款机构和企业，从 B2C 变成 B2B。企业签了 letters of intent，但产品做好后迟迟不付钱，原来他们不是 early adopters。David 为此多招了销售和工程师，结果只能裁员。(p.154-155)
   - **Platform pivot**：参考 Google AdWords，做了一个自助平台，任何人刷信用卡就能用。新产品只用一个月，注册 51%，激活 92%，留存 28%，推荐 64%，11% 的用户愿意每条消息付 20 美分。高推荐率意味着这是 viral engine of growth。(p.155-156)

5. **MVP 越来越快。** Votizen 的 MVP 周期依次是八个月、四个月、三个月、一个月。这不完全是因为已有的代码可以复用，很多产品在 pivot 之间被丢掉了，剩下的变成 legacy product 反而要额外改造。真正让速度提升的是每个 milestone 学到的关于客户、市场和战略的知识。后来 Votizen 从 Peter Thiel 那里融了 150 万美元。(p.156-157)

6. **Runway 是还能做几次 pivot。** 通常 runway 等于银行存款除以每月 burn rate，比如 100 万美元、每月花 10 万，就是十个月。缺钱时可以削减成本或融资，但盲目削减可能拖慢 Build-Measure-Learn 循环，只会让公司死得更慢。作者认为真正的 runway 是剩下的 pivot 次数，所以延长 runway 的另一种方法是更快到达每次 pivot，用更低成本或更短时间获得同样的 validated learning。(p.157-158)

7. **Pivot 需要勇气。** 大多数 pivot 过的创业者都说希望早点决定。作者给了三个原因：
   - vanity metrics 让人活在自己的现实里，觉得没必要改变；
   - 假设不清晰时几乎不可能彻底失败，没有失败就没有动力做大改变；
   - 害怕。最怕的是 vision 还没得到真正的机会就被判定错误，这种恐惧让人抗拒 MVP 和 split test，等到 vision 完整呈现时往往已经没钱 pivot。(p.158-159)

8. **Path 的例子。** Path 由 Dave Morin、Dustin Mierau 和 Shawn Fanning 创办，创始人名气大，2010 年发布 MVP 后科技博客反应很负面，尤其不满好友上限 50 人（依据 Robin Dunbar 的研究）。团队忽略媒体批评，专注于真实用户的反馈，用户想要的功能甚至不在原本的路线图上。Path 后来融资 850 万美元，据报道拒绝了 Google 1 亿美元的收购。(p.159-160)

9. **定期召开 pivot or persevere meeting。** 需要 pivot 的信号是产品实验效果越来越差，以及大家感觉产品开发应该更有成效。因为这个决定很情绪化，作者建议提前定好会议，间隔在几周到几个月之间。产品团队要带来一直以来完整的优化结果，并和预期做对比，业务负责人要带来和现有及潜在客户的详细对话记录。IMVU 还邀请外部顾问参加。(p.161)

10. **Wealthfront 从游戏转向。** Wealthfront 最初叫 kaChing，是面向业余投资者的虚拟交易游戏，想从中找出有才华的业余基金经理。它有 value hypothesis 和 growth hypothesis（viral 加 freemium）两个假设。游戏吸引了超过 45 万玩家，但这是 vanity metric：最终只有 7 位业余经理合格，付费客户只有 14 个。定性调查发现，专业基金经理并不怕透明，反而苦于难以扩大业务；消费者则被虚拟和真实投资混在一起弄糊涂了。公司决定放弃游戏用户，转向让客户投资专业经理，但保留了最有价值的经理评估技术。之后平台资金超过 1.8 亿美元，有四十多位专业经理。(p.161-165)

11. **IMVU 没有及时 pivot。** IMVU 月收入超过 100 万美元，创建了两千多万个 avatar 之后，外界的建议变成了不要质疑成功、保持现状。但 early adopter 市场正在耗尽，主流客户要求更高，激活率和变现率下降，获客成本上升，增长停滞。他们需要的是 customer segment pivot，却因为相信 vanity metrics、不再用 learning milestones 问责而拖了太久。后来通过建立客户 archetype 和一次大规模改版恢复增长，到 2009 年年收入超过 2,500 万美元。(p.165-168)

12. **Pivot 类型目录。** Pivot 不是"改变"的同义词，而是一种专门用来检验新的基本假设的改变。(p.168-172)

    | 类型 | 含义 | 页码 |
    |---|---|---|
    | Zoom-in pivot | 原来的一个功能变成整个产品（如 Votizen） | p.169 |
    | Zoom-out pivot | 原来的整个产品变成更大产品中的一个功能 | p.169 |
    | Customer segment pivot | 产品解决了真实问题，但客户不是原来设想的那群人 | p.169 |
    | Customer need pivot | 原来要解决的问题不重要，但发现了相关的重要问题（如 Potbelly 从古董店变成三明治连锁） | p.169-170 |
    | Platform pivot | 从应用变成平台，或反过来 | p.170 |
    | Business architecture pivot | 在高利润低销量和低利润高销量两种架构之间切换（来自 Geoffrey Moore） | p.170 |
    | Value capture pivot | 改变获取价值的方式，它是产品假设的一部分，会影响整个业务 | p.171 |
    | Engine of growth pivot | 在 viral、sticky、paid 三种增长引擎之间切换 | p.171 |
    | Channel pivot | 用不同渠道交付同样的解决方案，比如改成直销 | p.171-172 |
    | Technology pivot | 用完全不同的技术实现同样的方案，多见于成熟企业 | p.172 |

13. **Pivot 是一个战略假设。** 人们熟悉的是 Southwest、Walmart、Microsoft、Starbucks 这些公司最终的成功战略，却不了解背后经历过哪些 pivot。模仿成功公司的类比很难判断是否正确，所以 pivot 应该被看作一个需要新 MVP 来检验的新战略假设。即使成功之后公司也要继续 pivot。Pivot 是 Lean Startup 的核心，让公司走错路时能发现并找到新方向。Part Two 到此结束，下一部分讲如何加速。(p.172-174)

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

Every entrepreneur eventually has to ask whether the current strategic hypothesis is making enough progress or whether a pivot is needed. A pivot is a structured course correction that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy, and engine of growth. Innovation accounting makes pivots faster, and pivots take courage and a regular decision process.

### Key Points

1. **What a pivot is.** A pivot is a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy, and engine of growth. Ries stresses that the Lean Startup is not a rigid formula. Vision, intuition, and judgment stay in the process. The scientific method helps by testing our theories repeatedly so our judgment improves. The biggest waste of creative potential is a mistaken decision to persevere. (p.147-148)

2. **Votizen's series of pivots.** David Binetti wanted to increase civic participation. His first MVP, a social network of verified voters, took about three months and $1,200. He named four leaps of faith: registration, activation, retention, and referral.
   - First results: 5 percent registered and 17 percent verified voter status, too few to measure retention or referral. (p.148-149)
   - Two more months and $5,000 of split testing and design work raised registration to 17 percent and activation to over 90 percent, but retention was only 5 percent and referral 4 percent. (p.149-150)
   - Three more months of optimization brought retention to 8 percent and referral to 6 percent. After eight months and $20,000, the growth model still did not work. (p.150)

3. **Why David avoided the land of the living dead.** He launched early and iterated, so he reached a pivot decision only eight months in, before sunk costs made it too hard. He had also written down his leap-of-faith questions with quantitative predictions, which kept him from declaring success after the fact even though activation and total usage looked good. Because he had skipped early PR, admitting failure did not mean public embarrassment. Ries adds that the launch-and-see approach only guarantees that you will see what happens. (p.151-152)

4. **Zoom-in, customer segment, and platform pivots.**
   - **Zoom-in pivot**: customers liked the idea and valued voter verification, but found the social network empty. David turned the product into @2gov, a social lobbying platform where users contact elected officials through existing networks like Twitter, and @2gov turns those messages into printed letters for Congress. The growth model shifted from sticky to paid. Registration rose to 42 percent, activation 83 percent, retention 21 percent, referral 54 percent, but fewer than 1 percent of activists would pay. Total spend reached $50,000 over twelve months. (p.152-153)
   - **Customer segment pivot**: same functionality, but aimed at large organizations, fund-raisers, and companies instead of consumers, moving from B2C to B2B. Companies signed letters of intent, then stalled when it was time to pay. They were not early adopters. David had hired extra sales staff and engineers and had to cut back. (p.154-155)
   - **Platform pivot**: inspired by Google AdWords, David built a self-serve platform anyone could use with a credit card. It took one month to build and showed 51 percent sign-up, 92 percent activation, 28 percent retention, 64 percent referral, and 11 percent willing to pay 20 cents per message. The high referral rate pointed to a viral engine of growth. (p.155-156)

5. **MVPs got faster.** Votizen's MVP cycles took eight months, then four, three, and one. Reused code explains only part of this, since much of the product was thrown away between pivots and what remained became a legacy product that needed extra work. The main reason was what David learned about customers, the market, and strategy at each milestone. Votizen later raised $1.5 million from Peter Thiel. (p.156-157)

6. **Runway is the number of pivots left.** Runway usually means cash divided by monthly burn rate, so $1 million at $100,000 a month gives ten months. Startups short on cash cut costs or raise money, but careless cuts can slow the Build-Measure-Learn loop and just make the company die more slowly. Ries argues the real runway is how many pivots remain, which suggests another way to extend it: reach each pivot faster by getting the same validated learning at lower cost or in less time. (p.157-158)

7. **Pivots take courage.** Most entrepreneurs who pivoted say they wish they had done it sooner. Ries gives three reasons:
   - vanity metrics let founders live in a private reality where change seems unnecessary;
   - with an unclear hypothesis, complete failure is nearly impossible, and without failure there is little push for radical change;
   - fear. The worst fear is that the vision will be judged wrong before it gets a real chance. That fear makes people resist MVPs and split tests, and by the time the full vision is tested, the money for a pivot is often gone. (p.158-159)

8. **The Path example.** Path was founded by Dave Morin, Dustin Mierau, and Shawn Fanning. Because the founders were well known, its 2010 MVP drew heavy and negative coverage from tech blogs, especially over its 50-friend limit based on Robin Dunbar's research. The team ignored the press and listened to actual users, who asked for features not on the original road map. Path later raised $8.5 million and reportedly turned down a $100 million offer from Google. (p.159-160)

9. **Hold a regular pivot or persevere meeting.** The signs that a pivot is needed are product experiments that keep getting less effective and a general sense that development should be more productive. Since the decision is emotional, schedule the meeting in advance, somewhere between every few weeks and every few months. Product development brings the complete history of optimization results over time, compared with expectations. Business leadership brings detailed notes from conversations with current and potential customers. IMVU also invited outside advisers. (p.161)

10. **Wealthfront pivots away from a game.** Wealthfront began as kaChing, a virtual trading game for amateur investors meant to find talented amateur fund managers. It tested a value hypothesis and a growth hypothesis (viral growth plus freemium). The game drew more than 450,000 players, a vanity metric, since only seven amateur managers qualified and only fourteen customers paid. Qualitative work showed that professional managers did not fear transparency and struggled to scale their businesses, while consumers were confused by mixing virtual and real portfolios. The company dropped the gamers and let customers invest with professional managers, keeping its manager-evaluation technology as the core. It later had over $180 million on the platform and more than forty professional managers. (p.161-165)

11. **IMVU's failure to pivot.** Once IMVU passed $1 million in monthly revenue and twenty million avatars, the advice shifted to staying the course because the approach had worked. Meanwhile the early adopter market was running out. Mainstream customers were less forgiving, activation and monetization fell, acquisition costs rose, and growth stalled. IMVU needed a customer segment pivot but waited too long because it trusted vanity metrics and had stopped using learning milestones. A customer archetype and a major redesign restored growth, and revenue passed $25 million a year by 2009. (p.165-168)

12. **A catalog of pivots.** A pivot is not a synonym for change. It is a specific kind of change that tests a new fundamental hypothesis. (p.168-172)

    | Type | Meaning | Page |
    |---|---|---|
    | Zoom-in pivot | A single feature becomes the whole product (Votizen) | p.169 |
    | Zoom-out pivot | The whole product becomes one feature of a larger product | p.169 |
    | Customer segment pivot | The product solves a real problem, but for different customers than planned | p.169 |
    | Customer need pivot | The original problem is not important, but a related important one is found (Potbelly went from antique store to sandwich chain) | p.169-170 |
    | Platform pivot | Switching from an application to a platform, or the reverse | p.170 |
    | Business architecture pivot | Switching between high margin, low volume and low margin, high volume (from Geoffrey Moore) | p.170 |
    | Value capture pivot | Changing how the company captures value, which is part of the product hypothesis and affects the whole business | p.171 |
    | Engine of growth pivot | Switching among the viral, sticky, and paid engines of growth | p.171 |
    | Channel pivot | Delivering the same solution through a different channel, such as selling direct | p.171-172 |
    | Technology pivot | Reaching the same solution with a completely different technology, more common in established companies | p.172 |

13. **A pivot is a strategic hypothesis.** People know the end strategies of Southwest, Walmart, Microsoft, and Starbucks but rarely the pivots that got them there. Analogies to famous companies are hard to judge, so a pivot should be treated as a new strategic hypothesis that needs a new MVP to test it. Companies keep pivoting even after early success. Pivoting is the heart of the Lean Startup and lets a company notice a wrong turn and find another path. This closes Part Two, and the next part turns to acceleration. (p.172-174)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pivot | A structured course correction that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about product, strategy, and engine of growth |
| Persevere | Staying with the current strategy because data shows enough progress |
| Land of the living dead | A company that survives but falls short of expectations while draining resources and energy |
| Runway | Usually cash divided by burn rate; Ries redefines it as the number of pivots left |
| Burn rate | Net cash spent per month |
| Pivot or persevere meeting | A scheduled, recurring decision meeting with both product and business leadership |
| Legacy product | Existing product that no longer fits the company's goals but still needs upkeep or rework |
| Letter of intent | A statement of interest to buy; Votizen shows it is not the same as a sale |
| Value hypothesis / growth hypothesis | The two core assumptions about whether a product creates value and how it will grow |

### Discussion Questions

- What did Votizen keep and what did it change in each pivot? Which part stayed closest to its vision?
- If you measured a project you are working on by pivots remaining, how much runway would it have? How could you reach the next pivot faster?
- IMVU missed a pivot while it was succeeding. What signals during a period of success should warn a team that it needs to change direction?
