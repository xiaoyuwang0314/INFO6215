> Part Three: ACCELERATE ｜ PDF p.217-243

## 中文版

### 一句话总结

Startup 在成长中需要流程，但流程应该根据实际遇到的问题按比例逐步建立。Five Whys 就是这样一种机制，它能让团队在出问题时自动放慢、投资预防，问题减少后再自然提速。

### 核心观点

1. **公司变了，你的工作也悄悄变了。** 作者在 IMVU 当 CTO 时，有几次突然意识到自己已经在失职：公司长大了，同样的职位其实变成了一份新工作，而且没人通知你。他知道公司需要更多流程，但也见过很多 startup 为了显得"专业"而变得僵化和官僚。(p.217-218)

2. **两种失败和"折中"的陷阱。** 一种是 overarchitecture，想预防所有问题，结果迟迟发不出产品；另一种是 Friendster effect，用户暴涨时出现重大技术故障。常见建议是"做一点规划，但别太多"，但这种折中没有依据，显得老板很随意。下属会因此故意提出最极端的主张，好让折中结果接近自己想要的，最后大家越来越两极化。管理者要为这种激励负责。(p.218-219)

3. **Adaptive organization。** 作者以前认为 startup 不需要新员工培训项目，但 IMVU 最后建起了一个很好的培训体系，新人第一天就能产出。每个新工程师有一个 mentor，两人的绩效绑定在一起。这个体系不是某天决定要做的，而是在不断改进流程的过程中自然长出来的。作者把能根据当前情况自动调整流程和表现的组织叫 adaptive organization。(p.219)

4. **速度需要调节器，质量不能拿时间换。** 只追求速度是有害的，startup 需要内置的 speed regulator，andon cord 就是一个例子。Lean manufacturing 的重要发现是：现在制造或漏掉的质量问题，以后会让你更慢，因为缺陷带来返工、士气低落和客户投诉。服务业也一样，操作手册质量越高，越容易演进；质量差的手册里满是矛盾的规则，一改就乱。(p.219-220)

5. **MVP 和质量的矛盾。** 一方面，MVP 的逻辑是尽快给客户，多做的都是浪费；另一方面，Build-Measure-Learn 是持续循环，今天在质量、设计、基础设施上走的捷径，明天可能拖慢你。IMVU 早期满是 bug 的产品被客户拒绝，幸好没花时间修。但后来面对主流客户时，他们远不如 early adopters 宽容，功能越多，新功能与旧功能冲突的风险也越大。(p.220-221)

6. **Five Whys 的来源和原理。** 如何判断培训值不值得投入？自上而下很难估算。Five Whys 把投资直接和最严重问题的预防挂钩。它由 Toyota Production System 之父 Taiichi Ohno 发展成系统方法：连问五次"为什么"，找到根本原因。在 Ohno 的机器停机例子里，原因从保险丝烧断一路追到没装过滤器，也就是从技术故障追到了人的问题。作者认为每个看似技术的问题背后都有一个人的问题。(p.221-223)

7. **IMVU 的例子：从服务器故障到管理问题。** 新版本导致一个功能失效，原因是服务器故障；服务器故障是因为一个冷门子系统被用错了；用错是因为工程师不会用；不会用是因为没受过培训；没培训是因为他的经理觉得团队"太忙"。一个技术故障很快变成了管理问题。(p.223-224)

8. **Proportional investment（按比例投资）。** 在五个层级上都做投资，问题小就投小，问题大就投大。上例中要修服务器、改子系统让它更不容易出错、培训工程师，还要和经理谈。如果问题很小，就只做八周培训计划的第一个小时。问题再出现，就继续推进；不再出现，损失也只是一小时。IMVU 从没停下一切专门做培训，而是不断小步改进，最终省下了大量救火的时间。(p.224-225)

9. **自动的速度调节器。** 问题越多，投入解决问题的就越多；投资见效后，危机减少，团队又会提速。这样可以防止团队为了速度牺牲质量，也让进度和学习挂钩。遇到技术故障、业务结果不达标或客户行为意外变化时，都应该做 Five Whys。它和 small batches 结合，能让公司快速应对问题，又不过度投资或过度设计。(p.225)

10. **小心变成 Five Blames。** 团队刚用 Five Whys 时，容易变成互相指责。Five Whys 的目标是看到长期问题出在流程上，而不是人身上。对策是让所有受影响的人都到场，包括接电话的客服、修过问题的人和参与升级决策的高管，因为谁缺席谁就容易成为替罪羊。出现指责时，最资深的人应该说：出了错，是我们让这个错误太容易发生了。IMVU 让新工程师第一天就改生产环境，弄坏了就由他牵头修复和预防。(p.226-227)

11. **怎样开始。** Five Whys 需要互信和授权。条件不具备时，可以先用简化版两条规则：第一次犯的错都要宽容；同样的错不能犯第二次。简化版长期不够用，因为会引出"什么算同一个问题"之类的疑问，最终需要完整的 Five Whys。它会揭示组织里让人不舒服的事实，所以必须有足够权威的高管支持并担任裁判。个人如果没有上级支持，可以先对自己用两条规则。(p.227-229)

12. **从小处、具体处开始，并指定 Five Whys master。** 先选一类范围窄、不直接影响客户的问题（作者第一次成功用在一个内部测试工具上），因为高风险问题容易变成 Five Blames。触发规则要简单明确，比如"凡涉及信用卡交易的投诉都开会"，每层只做简单改动。每个领域指定一位 Five Whys master 主持会议、决定预防措施、分配后续任务，他要足够资深但又能出席会议。(p.229-230)

13. **IGN 的实践。** IGN 是 News Corporation 旗下的游戏媒体公司。第一次尝试时，他们拿一堆历史遗留问题开会，相关的人都不在，会议频繁跑题，效果很差。教训有三条：在新问题出现时做，旧问题会顺带浮现；所有相关的人必须到场；每次开始先简单解释流程。后来他们指定工程总监 Tony Ford 为 master，起初也因为问题选得太宽而受挫，直到一次关于项目延期的会议才真正成功。(p.230-233)

14. **IGN 的一次完整分析。** 博客无法发文，追下去是 API 报错，再到一个 gem 版本冲突，再到没有测试，再到 gem 管理没有自动化，最后追加一问：为什么周五晚上改生产环境。每一层都有具体负责人和对应的投资，例如改用 bundler、补测试、把 gem 管理纳入持续集成和部署，以及周末禁止生产变更。Tony 认为一次好的 Five Whys 有两个产出：学习和行动。(p.233-235)

15. **Intuit QuickBooks 第一年：achieving failure。** QuickBooks 一直按年度 waterfall 流程开发：前三四个月规划，之后六到九个月开发，到 beta 时设计已经锁定。2009 年 Greg Wright 加入团队，那年新的网银功能技术上没毛病，但客户对账时间变成旧版的四到五倍，Net Promoter Score 下降 20 分，修复又花了九个月。(p.236-238)

16. **第二年：组织有 muscle memory。** Greg 提出四个原则：更小的团队、更短的周期、更快的客户反馈、授权团队快速决策。他把发布节点提前到年中，但团队原定 1 月的 alpha 拖到 4 月，流程也慢慢变回老样子。单独改一个发布日期敌不过整个系统。(p.238-239)

17. **第三年：彻底重来。** Greg 和产品开发负责人 Himanshu Baxi 公开宣布抛弃旧流程，投资于能支持小批量的流程、产品和技术。年初不做完整路线图，而是开 idea/code/solution jams，把工程师、产品经理和客户放在一起。团队组成跨职能小组，客户从每个功能构思时就参与。部分 PM 和工程师起初抵触，领导层靠反复沟通推动变化。以前网银团队有 15 名工程师和 7 名 QA，现在每个团队不超过 5 人，代码分支从 5 个增加到二十多个，每个功能大约六周，全程和真实客户测试。(p.239-241)

18. **文化之外还需要技术投入。** QuickBooks 存着小企业的关键财务数据，团队不敢发布有风险的 MVP。他们开发了虚拟化系统，让新版本能读取客户数据但不能永久修改，这样就能安全地让部分真实客户试用。第三年发布的版本满意度明显更高，销量也更好。之后他们开始尝试订阅模式，计划按季度发布。(p.241-242)

19. **从 startup 到成熟公司。** 用 lean 方法成长起来的 startup 已经习惯纪律、定制流程、Five Whys 和 small batches，更容易形成 Toyota 那样的执行文化。但成长为大公司不是终点，成熟公司也必须通过颠覆式创新寻找新增长，而且这种压力来得越来越早。企业必须同时追求 operational excellence 和 disruptive innovation，这需要第 12 章讲的 portfolio 思维。(p.242-243)

## English Version

### One-Sentence Summary

A growing startup needs process, but that process should be built gradually and in proportion to the problems it actually hits. The Five Whys does this by slowing a team down to invest in prevention when problems appear and letting it speed up again once they are fixed.

### Key Points

1. **The company changes, and so does your job.** As CTO of IMVU, Ries realized a few times that he was already failing at his job. The company had grown, so the same title had quietly become a new job, and nobody told him. He knew the company needed more process, but he had also seen startups turn rigid and bureaucratic in an effort to look "professional." (p.217-218)

2. **Two failure modes and the split-the-difference trap.** One failure is overarchitecture, where trying to prevent every possible problem delays the product. The other is the Friendster effect, a high-profile technical failure just as adoption takes off. The usual advice is to plan a little but not too much, yet there is no rationale for which problems to anticipate, so the boss looks arbitrary. People then argue for extreme positions so the average lands near what they want, and everyone polarizes over time. Managers are responsible for creating those incentives. (p.218-219)

3. **The adaptive organization.** Ries once thought training programs were only for big companies, yet IMVU built one so good that new hires were productive on day one. Each new engineer had a mentor, and their performance was linked. Nobody decided one day to build a great program. It grew out of steadily improving the process. Ries calls this an adaptive organization, one that adjusts its process and performance to current conditions automatically. (p.219)

4. **Speed needs a regulator, and quality cannot be traded for time.** Focusing on speed alone is destructive, so startups need built-in speed regulators like the andon cord. A key lean finding is that quality problems you cause or miss now will slow you down later through rework, low morale, and complaints. Service businesses are the same: a high-quality playbook is easier to evolve, while a poor one is full of contradictory rules that cause confusion whenever something changes. (p.219-220)

5. **The MVP versus quality paradox.** The MVP logic says get the product to customers fast and treat extra work as waste. But Build-Measure-Learn is continuous, so shortcuts in quality, design, or infrastructure today can slow you down tomorrow. IMVU's early buggy product was rejected, and it was good they had not polished it. Later, mainstream customers were far less forgiving than early adopters, and each new feature risked breaking existing ones. (p.220-221)

6. **Where the Five Whys comes from.** Deciding top-down whether training is worth it means estimating two unknowns. The Five Whys ties investment directly to preventing the most painful symptoms. Taiichi Ohno, father of the Toyota Production System, developed it into a systematic tool: ask "why" five times to reach the root cause. In Ohno's example of a stopped machine, the cause moves from a blown fuse to a missing strainer, from a technical fault to a human one. Ries argues that every seemingly technical problem has a human problem underneath. (p.221-223)

7. **An IMVU example.** A new release disabled a feature because a server failed. The server failed because an obscure subsystem was misused. It was misused because the engineer did not know how to use it. He did not know because he was never trained. He was never trained because his manager thought the team was "too busy." A technical fault turned into a management issue. (p.223-224)

8. **Proportional investment.** Invest at each of the five levels, small for minor symptoms and large for painful ones. In the example, that means fixing the server, making the subsystem less error-prone, training the engineer, and talking to the manager. For a minor glitch, do only the first hour of an eight-week training plan. If the problem recurs, keep going. If it does not, you lost an hour. IMVU never dropped everything for training, yet steady small improvements compounded and freed time once lost to firefighting. (p.224-225)

9. **An automatic speed regulator.** More problems mean more investment in solutions, and as those pay off, crises drop and the team speeds up. This keeps teams from trading quality for speed and ties progress to learning. Teams should run a Five Whys after any failure: technical faults, missed business results, or unexpected customer behavior. Combined with small batches, it lets a company respond fast without overinvesting or overengineering. (p.225)

10. **Beware the Five Blames.** New teams often turn the Five Whys into finger-pointing. The goal is to see that chronic problems come from bad process, not bad people. Everyone affected should be in the room, including customer service reps, anyone who tried a fix, and executives involved in escalation, because whoever is absent becomes the scapegoat. When blame surfaces, the most senior person should say that if a mistake happened, it is on us for making it so easy to make. IMVU had new engineers change production on day one, and anyone who broke it led the fix and the prevention work. (p.226-227)

11. **Getting started.** The Five Whys needs mutual trust and empowerment. Without them, start with two simple rules: be tolerant of every mistake the first time, and never allow the same mistake twice. The simple version does not hold up long term because it raises questions like what counts as the same problem, so eventually the full method is needed. It will expose unpleasant truths, so an executive with enough authority must sponsor it and act as referee. Individual contributors without manager buy-in can apply the two rules to their own work. (p.227-229)

12. **Start small, be specific, and appoint a Five Whys master.** Begin with a narrow class of symptoms that does not directly affect customers (Ries's first success was on an internal testing tool), since high-stakes problems quickly turn into Five Blames. Use a simple, unambiguous trigger rule, such as holding a session for every complaint involving a credit card transaction, and make simple changes at each level. Appoint a Five Whys master for each area to run meetings, choose prevention steps, and assign follow-up. The master should be senior enough to get things done but available to attend. (p.229-230)

13. **IGN's experience.** IGN is a video game media company owned by News Corporation. Its first attempt tackled a list of long-standing "baggage" problems, none of the most knowledgeable people attended, and the meeting kept drifting off topic. Three lessons followed: run sessions as new problems arise and let old issues surface on their own, include everyone connected to the problem, and open each session by explaining the process. IGN then named engineering director Tony Ford as master. He also struggled at first because he did not pick a narrow focus, until a session on a project missing its deadlines finally worked. (p.230-233)

14. **A full IGN analysis.** Blog posts could not be added or edited. The chain went from API errors to a gem version conflict, to missing tests, to gem management that was not automated, plus a bonus question about why production changes happened on Friday nights. Each level had a named owner and a matching investment, such as switching to bundler, adding tests, automating gem installation in continuous integration and deployment, and banning weekend production changes. Tony says a good Five Whys produces two outputs, learning and doing. (p.233-235)

15. **QuickBooks year one: achieving failure.** Intuit built QuickBooks on an annual waterfall cycle: three or four months of planning, six to nine months of building, and a design locked by beta. Greg Wright joined in 2009. That year's new online banking feature was technically sound, but reconciling transactions took customers four to five times longer than before, the Net Promoter Score fell 20 points, and the fix took nine months. (p.236-238)

16. **Year two: organizations have muscle memory.** Greg set four principles: smaller teams, shorter cycle times, faster customer feedback, and empowered teams that decide quickly. He moved to a midyear release milestone, but the alpha planned for January slipped to April, and the process drifted back to its old shape. Changing a release date alone could not beat the system. (p.238-239)

17. **Year three: starting over.** Greg and product development leader Himanshu Baxi publicly threw out the old processes and invested in process, product, and technology changes that allowed small batches. Instead of a full-year road map, they ran idea/code/solution jams with engineers, product managers, and customers. Teams became cross-functional, and customers joined from the start of each feature. Some PMs and engineers resisted, and leaders relied on constant communication. The old online banking team had fifteen engineers and seven QA specialists. Now no team exceeds five people, branches grew from five to twenty or more, and each feature takes about six weeks with real customers testing throughout. (p.239-241)

18. **Culture change needs technical investment too.** QuickBooks holds critical financial data for small businesses, so the team feared releasing a risky MVP. They built a virtualization system that let a new version read customer data without changing it permanently, so real customers could test safely. That year's release had higher satisfaction and sold more units. The team then began experimenting with subscriptions and planned to release quarterly. (p.241-242)

19. **From startup to established company.** Startups that grow with lean methods already know discipline, tailored process, the Five Whys, and small batches, which positions them for a Toyota-like culture of execution. Growing up is not the end, though. Established companies must also find new growth through disruptive innovation, and that pressure now arrives earlier. Companies must pursue operational excellence and disruptive innovation at once, which calls for the portfolio thinking of Chapter 12. (p.242-243)

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Adaptive organization | An organization that adjusts its process and performance to current conditions automatically |
| Overarchitecture failure | Trying to prevent every problem and delaying the product as a result |
| Friendster effect | A high-profile technical failure just as customer adoption takes off |
| Speed regulator | A built-in mechanism that helps teams find their best pace |
| Five Whys | Taiichi Ohno's root cause method of asking "why" five times |
| Proportional investment | Prevention effort scaled to the severity of the problem, made at all five levels |
| Five Blames | A Five Whys session that turns into finger-pointing |
| Five Whys master | The person who moderates sessions, picks prevention steps, and assigns follow-up |
| Waterfall | A linear, large-batch development process that depends on forecasting and planning |
| Net Promoter Score (NPS) | The tracking survey Intuit uses to measure customer satisfaction |
| Muscle memory | Old organizational habits that are hard to unlearn |

### Discussion Questions

- Take a recent problem in a group project and ask "why" five times. Did you end up at a technical cause or a human one?
- Could your team follow "tolerate every mistake once, never allow it twice"? What would make it hard?
- Why did changing the release date alone fail in QuickBooks' second year? What does that suggest about changing your own study habits?
